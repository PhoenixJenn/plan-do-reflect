Homepage narrative — "One Cycle, Every Altitude"
→ index.html
Drafted 2026-08-18

How the Plan-Do-Reflect framework scales from individual use up through team and corporate use — with the framing that at scale, Reflect becomes more of an Inspect.

One Cycle, Every Altitude

Plan-Do-Reflect starts small: one person decides what matters, does the work, then looks back and adjusts before doing it again. That's the whole cycle at its most honest — no committee, no dashboard, just a person and a notebook.

The same three motions don't disappear once other people get involved. They just get formal names and more people in the room. A team's Plan is a product roadmap or a Project Charter. Its Do is a sprint, a build, a release. And its Reflect is a retro, a post-mortem, a status report — the same "what actually happened, what do we do differently" question, just asked out loud in a meeting instead of in a journal.

Zoom out to the level of a whole company and the pattern holds again: Plan becomes the Annual Operating Plan. Do becomes a year of execution across every team. And Reflect becomes something slightly different — call it Inspect. The shift in name isn't decoration. Personal reflection is introspective: you're the only one deciding what it meant. A Monthly or Quarterly Business Review is not that. Nobody's journaling in an MBR — you're inspecting a shared set of numbers against a plan everyone in the room can also see, and the review itself is the mechanism, not a private habit. Same function, look back, be honest about the gap, decide what's next, just run as a scheduled, collective ritual instead of a solitary one.

That's the actual claim underneath this whole site: Plan-Do-Reflect isn't a personal productivity trick with a corporate coat of paint over it. It's the same operating logic at every altitude, from a Sunday planning session up to a board-level QBR. The only thing that changes at scale is who reflection answers to — at some point it stops being something you do alone, and starts being something you do in front of a room.

What makes a vision statement good
→ product-management.html
Drafted 2026-08-18

A checklist for judging a Vision Statement, not just writing one — five traits a strong vision has that a weak one doesn't. Candidate addition to the Vision Statement field on Product Vision & Strategy, not folded in yet.

  • Clear — states the future plainly enough that it doesn't need explaining. If it takes a paragraph to unpack, it isn't clear yet.
  • Big — stretches the team's thinking past what's already true today, not a polished description of the current roadmap.
  • Inspiring — makes people want to work toward it, not just nod along and accept it.
  • Specific — specific enough that it actually helps decide what to build next, not so abstract it could describe any company in the category.
  • Transformational — describes a real change in how people live or work, not just "faster/better/cheaper" version of the status quo.

Run against the iPod example already on the site: "Every song you own, in your pocket, always with you" is clear, big relative to 2001's players, inspiring, specific enough to rule out a phone or a video player, and transformational — it changed how people related to their music library, not just the device.

Business models — archetypes, revenue types, and model vs. strategy
→ candidate: Business Acumen
Drafted 2026-08-18

Four related ideas about how a business actually makes money — useful background for Product Vision & Strategy's Differentiation and Strategic Bet fields, even though this content itself belongs somewhere else.

Two archetypes

Product-based: identify a need → design and build → market and sell → deliver value → scale. Wins through repeatable revenue and high scalability — one product can reach millions. Apple is the standard example: it doesn't just sell devices, it sells an ecosystem (iPhone, Mac, AirPods, Watch) that keeps switching costs high.

Service-based: understand the need → offer expertise → deliver consistently → create value and grow. Wins through relationships and trust rather than a shelf product — revenue is recurring because the client keeps paying for outcomes, not a one-time purchase.

Five common revenue models

  • Subscription — recurring pay, predictable revenue (Netflix, Spotify).
  • Marketplace — connects buyers and sellers, earns on commission (Airbnb, Etsy).
  • Freemium — free core product, paid upgrade (Dropbox, LinkedIn).
  • Advertising — free to the user, monetized through attention (Google, YouTube).
  • Direct-to-consumer — sell straight to the customer, no middleman, higher margin.

No model is "best" — it's a fit question: understand the customer, solve a real problem, then pick the model that fits how that value actually gets captured.

Model vs. strategy — a useful distinction

A business model explains how you create and capture value (customers → value proposition → operations → revenue). Strategy explains how you win (competitive advantage → strategic focus → growth approach → long-term value). A strong model with a weak strategy still loses, and vice versa. This maps cleanly onto Product Vision & Strategy's Differentiation and Strategic Bets fields.

Porter's Five Forces
→ candidate: Strategic Planning
Drafted 2026-08-18

A framework for reading the competitive pressure on a business, not just naming direct competitors. Strategic Planning's own description on core-skills.html — "scan the landscape, formulate, cascade into plans, measure, adjust" — the five forces are literally the "scan the landscape" step.

  • Threat of new entrants — how easily fresh competitors can show up and undercut on price.
  • Supplier power — how much suppliers can squeeze you on price or quality if you're dependent on them.
  • Buyer power — how much customers can demand lower prices or better terms.
  • Threat of substitutes — alternative ways to solve the same problem that aren't direct competitors.
  • Rivalry among existing competitors — the force at the center, shaped by the other four.

The lesson worth keeping: the force actually hurting you isn't always the obvious one. A café losing customers isn't necessarily losing them to other cafés — it might be losing them to delivery apps (a substitute), and the fix is competing on experience and loyalty, not price.

Market size — TAM, SAM, SOM
→ product-management.html
Drafted 2026-08-18

Three narrowing circles for sizing an opportunity, from the whole addressable market down to what's actually winnable.

  • TAM (Total Addressable Market) — total demand for the product or service globally, no limits. Shows the size of the opportunity.
  • SAM (Serviceable Available Market) — the slice of TAM you can actually target, given geography, audience, or business model. Defines the real playing field.
  • SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market) — the realistic share of SAM you can capture with current resources, strategy, and time. Sets the actual goal.

Good fit for Product Vision & Strategy's Why Now and Problem/Opportunity fields — TAM/SAM/SOM is the quantitative backup for a qualitative "why now" claim.

Emotional vs. rational buying
→ candidate: Research
Drafted 2026-08-18

People decide emotionally and justify rationally, not the other way around — worth keeping in mind for Differentiation and any customer-facing positioning work.

  • Emotional drivers — feeling, status/aspiration, impulse/excitement, and brand identity.
  • Rational drivers — research/comparison, features and quality, price/value, and practical need.

The two work in sequence, not competition: an emotional trigger creates interest, a rational check builds trust, the decision gets made, and satisfaction afterward is what creates loyalty. Strong positioning gives people both — an emotional reason to want it, and a rational reason to justify it.

Asset-light businesses
→ candidate: Business Acumen
Drafted 2026-08-18

A growth pattern built on access instead of ownership — worth pairing with the business-model note above, since it's really a variant of how a business model captures value.

  • Idea & value creation — start from a real problem, not an asset you already own.
  • Leverage partnerships — use other people's assets, networks, and platforms instead of buying your own.
  • Scale smarter — technology and systems drive efficiency instead of headcount or inventory.
  • Grow faster — lower costs and higher flexibility let the business scale faster than an asset-heavy competitor could.

The trade-off it's naming: asset-heavy means high upfront investment, slower scaling, high fixed costs, and less flexibility; asset-light trades that for lower upfront investment, faster scaling, and more flexibility to change course. Airbnb is the standard example — it built a global hospitality business without owning a single hotel room, connecting hosts and travelers instead. The underlying claim: access is more powerful than ownership.

Subscription business model, in more depth
→ candidate: Business Acumen
Drafted 2026-08-18

Expands the one-line "Subscription" entry in the business-models note above — how it actually works and what makes one succeed or fail, not just the definition.

How it works

Customer signs up → gets charged automatically on a recurring basis → receives ongoing access or new content/updates → sticks around, which drives down churn and increases lifetime value. The loop only works if step three (continuous value) keeps happening — a subscription that stops delivering new value collapses back to step four early.

What separates success from failure

  • Succeeds by: delivering real value consistently, staying close to what customers actually need, continuously improving the offering, and keeping plans flexible with easy cancellation.
  • Fails by: overcomplicating pricing, ignoring the customer experience, or letting the "continuous value" part quietly stop happening.

The reframe worth keeping: a subscription isn't a billing mechanism, it's a trust mechanism — you're asking someone to keep paying before they've seen what's next, which only works if they already believe you'll deliver.

How to explain a risk without sounding negative
→ project-program-management.html (Risk Management)
Drafted 2026-08-18

A six-step pattern for communicating a risk to stakeholders, not for identifying or logging it — this is about tone once the risk register already exists. Directly relevant to the Risk Management section already built on Project & Program Management, which covers how to log and score a risk but not how to talk about it.

  • Be clear and specific — name the risk and what it could actually impact, not a vague gesture at "concerns."
  • Focus on the outcome, not the problem — describe what might happen, not what's currently wrong.
  • Provide context and perspective — say how likely and how significant it actually is, so it doesn't read as bigger than it is.
  • Balance it with the benefits — show why the path is still worth taking despite the risk.
  • Share how to manage it — give the practical steps already in motion to reduce or avoid it.
  • End with confidence, not alarm — leave the reader informed and able to act, not anxious.

Good match for the Status Report Template's Key Risks & Issues field — that section already asks for a risk name, severity, and mitigation; this adds the tone discipline for how to phrase all three.

Enterprise Architect vs. Business Architect
→ personal reference, not for site
Drafted 2026-08-18

Captured for personal reference, not queued for the site. The core distinction: it's not about where either role sits in an org chart, it's about what each one architects.

Business Architect

Designs how a single business domain works — capabilities, value streams, processes, roles & responsibilities, business information. Question they're answering: how should this part of the business work?

Enterprise Architect

Connects domains, creates end-to-end coherence, sees the dependencies between them, aligns business and IT, balances what's good locally against what's good for the enterprise, and translates strategy into actual change. Question they're answering: how does the enterprise work as one?

The value chain both roles sit inside

Strategy → Capabilities → Processes → Information → Applications → Technology → Change.

Where it actually goes wrong

A great functional design can still fail if the data, applications, technology, and wider enterprise around it don't support it — each domain optimized in isolation, disconnected in reality. The real distinction isn't seniority: Business Architecture creates coherence within a domain; Enterprise Architecture creates coherence across domains, connected and working as one instead of four separate optimizations.

The habits behind better financial decisions
→ candidate: Business Acumen
Drafted 2026-08-18

The core claim: a business gets the results of its financial habits whether or not those habits were ever chosen on purpose. Five habits that separate businesses that prepare for decisions from businesses that just react to events:

  • Review meaningful numbers before deciding — not after.
  • Monitor cash flow before it becomes a problem — not once it already is one.
  • Discuss performance regularly — not just at month-end.
  • Follow controls consistently — especially when busy, which is exactly when they get skipped.
  • Base decisions on timely information — not on assumptions filling the gap where current data should be.

The reframe worth keeping: the difference isn't effort, it's habit quality — reacting vs. preparing. The practical version: pick one financial habit, schedule it, protect it, repeat it, and make it part of a weekly rhythm rather than something that only happens when someone remembers.

Mastering the P&L — financial fluency for product managers
→ candidate: Product Management
Drafted 2026-08-18

A practical framework for translating product work into dollar terms — the skill that actually gets an initiative funded. More specific to PMs than the earlier Business Acumen batch; this reads like a missing skill inside Product Management itself, not general business literacy.

Five-step framework

  • Identify the P&L lever — which line item does this actually touch: Revenue, COGS, OpEx, or Margin?
  • Quantify the baseline — what's the current state before you touch anything?
  • Build the impact model — the math connecting a product change to an actual dollar number.
  • Account for the true cost — what does it actually cost to build and run, not just build?
  • Speak finance language — translate the pitch into the numbers a finance team already trusts.

The impact model

(Users Affected) × (Behavior Change) × (Dollar Value) = P&L Impact. A conversion-rate lift across a large user base, multiplied by average order value, turns "this will help" into a specific revenue number a finance team can act on.

True cost, not just build cost

Easy to forget: engineering time, infrastructure and hosting, increased support load, ongoing maintenance, and opportunity cost — what you're not building instead. Net Impact = Revenue Impact − Total Costs, not just Revenue Impact on its own.

Translating PM speak into finance speak

Vague claims ("improves user experience," "increases engagement," "users want this," "we need to modernize") don't survive a funding conversation. Dollar-quantified claims do: "increases ARPU by $X," "reduces churn by X%, protecting $Y in ARR," "reduces infrastructure cost by $X/year." The pattern is the same every time — swap the adjective for a formula.

The test before you pitch anything

Five questions to answer before pitching an initiative: which P&L line does it touch, what's the dollar value of that impact, what's the total cost to build and run it, what's the payback period, and what's the opportunity cost. Can't answer all five yet? Don't pitch it yet.

Customer-led product group expansion — a four-stage process
→ candidate: split across Research, Product Management, and Go-to-Market
Drafted 2026-08-18

A four-stage process for expanding into a new customer-led product line, validating demand at every stage before committing real investment. It maps across three things — more than it fits into any one section we've scoped.

1. Understand Customer Needs

Talk to customers at scale to find real opportunities: discover what else they're already buying elsewhere, identify what needs improving, quantify how much they'd spend, and validate what would actually make them switch. This is Research territory — the deep methodology our Understanding Your Customers section on Product Management deliberately doesn't try to replace.

2. Design the Right Solution

Turn that insight into a validated concept: define a spec based on customer input, set a realistic price point, build a prototype, and re-test it with the same customers. Overlaps our still-unbuilt Ideation & MVP Scoping and Product Requirements territory.

3. Confirm Demand Before Launch

Validate with the same customers before investing fully: present the spec, price, and availability, confirm they'd actually switch, measure committed volume, and refine based on what you hear. Also Research/validation territory — and the step most teams skip, which is how products end up launching to a market that was never actually confirmed.

4. Launch and Grow Margin

Launch to existing customers and grow profitably: sell more, improve margin, increase retention, drive growth. This is Go-to-Market territory, which isn't in our taxonomy at all yet — we already ruled GTM out of Product Management's own scope when we first outlined it.

The lesson worth keeping regardless of where the content eventually lands: validate before you build, and validate again before you launch. Most of the risk in a new product line isn't the build — it's skipping stage 1 or stage 3.

The iPod's actual origin story — more accurate than our example implies
→ product-management.html
Drafted 2026-08-18

The popular "Steve Jobs had a vision" story isn't quite how it started. The initiative began with Jon Rubinstein, Apple's head of hardware, who in early 2001 noticed two enabling pieces had just become available at the same time: Toshiba had built a new 1.8-inch, 5GB hard drive and was looking for a use for it, and PortalPlayer had a reference design for a digital-music-player chipset that hadn't found a taker. Rubinstein saw those two pieces fit together into a real opportunity, pitched Jobs, and Jobs approved the project.

So the actual origin was components-driven, not "Apple decided to go solve portable music friction" from a blank page. The friction-fixing mission became real once the project was already approved — the existing MP3 players on the market in 2000-2001 really were bad (clunky interfaces, a few dozen songs of storage, slow USB 1.1 transfers), and Apple's insight from there was to design the whole experience end to end: device, scroll wheel, and iTunes together, with FireWire for fast syncing. "1,000 songs in your pocket" was explicitly a jab at how slow and limited competitors were. Tony Fadell was brought in as an outside contractor to lead the actual product development, working under Rubinstein with a small team over about eight to nine months to ship by October 2001 — often credited as the key architect who turned the concept into a real product.

Both things are true, just in a particular order: an enabling piece of hardware created the opening, and the "fix this bad experience" mission shaped everything that got built once the team had committed. Credit has been genuinely disputed over the years between Fadell, Rubinstein, and Jobs — Apple's own later marketing folded it into the broader "visionary Steve Jobs" story in a way that flattens the components-first origin.
