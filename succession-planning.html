Succession Planning

Who's ready to grow into what — decided before you need the answer, not scrambled together the week someone resigns.

Try It
1

Succession Planning vs. Replacement Planning

The two get used interchangeably, but they answer different questions — and only one of them is actually planning.

Replacement Planning

Reactive: who covers this role tomorrow if the person in it leaves? Usually just a backup name next to a box on an org chart — a contingency, not a development plan.

Succession Planning

Proactive: who are we deliberately growing toward a role they're not in yet? Built over months or years, with real development work behind it — not just a name on a list.

The 9-Box Grid

The standard tool for sorting people by two separate questions that get conflated constantly: how are they doing right now (performance), and how much room do they have to grow (potential). A star performer isn't automatically a succession candidate — and a high-potential person isn't automatically delivering yet.

3

The Process

Succession planning fails when it's a one-time exercise before a reorg instead of a standing cadence.

1

Identify the critical roles — not every role, just the ones where an unplanned vacancy would genuinely hurt. Be honest about which ones those actually are.

2

Identify candidates — more than one per role where possible. A single named successor is still a single point of failure if they leave too.

3

Assess readiness — ready now, ready in 1-2 years, ready in 3-5 years, or needs real development first. A different conversation and a different plan for each.

4

Build individual development plans — the actual stretch assignments, exposure, and coaching that close the specific gap for that specific person, not a generic training module.

5

Review on a cadence — readiness, roles, and candidates all change. A plan reviewed once and filed away is a replacement plan wearing a succession-planning label.

Watch-out

Two failure modes show up constantly. First, the single-threaded critical role — one person who's quietly become the only one who can do something, discovered only when they're already walking out the door. Second, the silent succession candidate — someone named internally as a future leader who's never actually told, and leaves for a role elsewhere that made the growth path explicit. A plan that exists only in a spreadsheet protects the org chart; it does nothing for retention unless the person on it knows where they stand.

Try It

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

Help me build a simple succession plan for my team. Ask me to name the 2-3 roles where an unplanned vacancy would genuinely hurt, then for each one ask who could step in and how ready they are (ready now, ready in 1-2 years, ready in 3-5 years, or needs real development). For anyone rated "ready now" or "1-2 years," ask whether they actually know they're being considered — and if not, push me on why.
