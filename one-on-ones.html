1:1s

The recurring meeting that does the most work in Managing — and the one most likely to quietly turn into a status update if nobody protects it.

Try It

The structure below follows Mark Horstman's O3 model from The Effective Manager — one of the few management frameworks built to be followed almost word for word rather than adapted on the fly.

1

The Structure

Thirty minutes, split three ways, in this order. Reversing the order is the single most common way a 1:1 stops being one.

1

Their priorities first — the direct sets the agenda, not the manager. Whatever's on their mind, in whatever order they bring it.

2

Your items second — a short, prepared list, not a running status check. If it's urgent enough to need more time than this, it's not a 1:1 topic.

3

Coaching last — forward-looking, not a recap. What's the one thing worth stretching toward before next week.

2

The Cadence

What erodes it

Cancelling when things get busy — which is exactly when a direct report needs it most. A 1:1 that moves every other week stops being trusted as a fixed point.

What protects it

Same day, same time, every week, treated with the same priority as a meeting with your own manager. If it has to move, reschedule it — don't just drop it.

3

Feedback In the Moment

Doesn't wait for the 1:1 or the Performance Review — a short, repeatable script for the moment it actually happens.

1

Ask permission — "Can I give you some feedback?" Takes two seconds and changes how the next part lands.

2

Describe the behavior — specific and observable, not a character judgment. What they did, not who they are.

3

State the impact — what actually happened as a result, so it's clear why it matters.

4

Ask for future behavior — "What do you think you'd do differently next time?" Lands as coaching, not a verdict.

4

Questions to Ask

Starting points for a slow week, not a script to read verbatim — the direct's own agenda always comes first.

Opening it up

  • "What's top of mind for you this week?"
  • "What's going well that we should do more of?"
  • "What's getting in your way right now?"

Getting past the surface — Julie Zhuo's structure, from The Making of a Manager

  • "What's the actual root cause of what's making that hard?"
  • "What would the ideal outcome here look like?"
  • "What can I do to help?"

Worth asking less often — monthly, not every week

  • "How are you feeling about where you're headed here?"
  • "What's one thing you want to get better at over the next few months?"
  • "Is there anything you need from me or the team that you're not getting?"
Watch-out

If you're setting the first two-thirds of the agenda, it's a status meeting wearing a 1:1's name. The fix isn't a better template — it's asking "what's on your mind?" and actually waiting.

Try It

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

Help me give a piece of feedback I've been sitting on. Ask me to describe the specific behavior — not a character judgment — and the actual impact it had. Then help me phrase it using ask-permission, describe-the-behavior, state-the-impact, ask-for-future-behavior, and push back if any part of what I've written reads like a verdict instead of an open question.
