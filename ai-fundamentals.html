AI Fundamentals

A learning path, not a single skill: understand it, learn to use it, apply it to your work, then make it habitual.

Try It

This page is for using AI well, not building it. If you want the engineering side — RAG, evals, guardrails, agents and harnesses — that's AI Beyond Fundamentals. This page stays at the level of a working professional deciding what to reach for and when.

1 · Understand It

An LLM (the "brain" behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini) predicts the next most likely piece of text, trained on an enormous amount of writing. It isn't a database that looks up facts — it's closer to a very well-read pattern-matcher that's extremely good at completing and transforming text. That explains both what it's great at and where it quietly breaks.

Genuinely strong at

  • Drafting, rephrasing and summarizing
  • Brainstorming and finding angles you hadn't considered
  • Explaining something in the exact terms you already know
  • Finding patterns across text you hand it directly

Quietly unreliable at

  • Stating facts with total confidence while being wrong ("hallucination")
  • Anything after its training cutoff, unless it's actually searching live
  • Checking its own work — it can't tell confident from correct
  • Math and precise counting, done in its head instead of with a tool

Words worth knowing

  • Model vs. tool — Claude, GPT, Gemini are the models (the brain); ChatGPT, Claude.ai, Gemini are the products built around them (the packaging). Same brand name is often used for both.
  • Context window — how much text it can actually "see" at once. A long conversation or a huge document can push earlier content out of view without any warning.
  • Prompt — what you give it: instructions, a question, a document, or all three.
  • Hallucination — a fluent, confident, wrong answer. The tone gives you no signal; only checking does.
  • Agent — not a bigger model, a model wired to take multiple actions toward a goal on its own instead of answering one question. More in Apply It to Your Work below.

2 · Learn to Use It

The gap between someone who's "tried ChatGPT" and someone who actually relies on it is almost never the model — it's a handful of habits.

Prompting, in practice

1

Say the goal, not just the task — "write a status update" gets a generic draft; "write a status update for my VP who skims, flagging the one risk that needs a decision" gets a usable one.

2

Hand it the context directly — paste the actual email thread, document, or data instead of describing it from memory. It can't see your inbox or your hard drive unless you give it access.

3

Specify the output shape — a bulleted list, a table, a specific word count. It will default to whatever's generic unless you say otherwise.

4

Treat the first answer as a draft — iterate ("shorter," "more direct," "push back on this part") instead of expecting a perfect first pass.

5

Ask it to ask you — when the task is fuzzy, tell it to ask clarifying questions before answering, rather than guessing at what you meant.

Features worth actually learning

Most people use one chat box and nothing else. A few features do a lot of the remaining work: uploading a file instead of pasting text, persistent custom instructions or a "project" so you're not re-explaining context every conversation, voice mode for thinking out loud, and image generation or analysis when the task isn't text.

Watch-out

Verify anything you'd be embarrassed to be wrong about — a number, a quote, a citation, a legal or medical claim — before you act on it or send it to someone else. Fluent isn't the same as checked.

3 · Apply It to Your Work

Once a single chat is a habit, the next step is usually wanting AI to do more without you babysitting every step — pull from your tools, take several actions, finish a multi-step task on its own. That's an agent, and there are four very different ways to get one, ranging from free and already in your hands to a real engineering project.

CategoryExamples todayBest forTradeoff
A vendor's built-in agent modeChatGPT's agent mode & Projects, Claude's Agent Skills, Gemini's GemsYou want the tool you already pay for to do moreSimplest and cheapest — nothing to buy or set up — but limited to what that one vendor's tool can reach
No-code agent buildern8n, Zapier AgentsWiring together tools you already use (Slack, Sheets, email, calendars) without writing codeMore flexible than a built-in agent mode, less powerful than a custom build; still real setup work
Off-the-shelf enterprise platformGlean, Moveworks, Salesforce Agentforce, Microsoft Copilot StudioYour problem is a known category — enterprise search, IT/HR tickets, CRMFastest way to a working rollout, but only as flexible as the vendor's templates, and locks you into that ecosystem
Build-your-own frameworkLangChain/LangGraph, CrewAI, AutoGen (AG2), OpenAI Agents SDK, Claude Agent SDKA workflow nobody sells, or one that has to live inside your own codebaseMost control and capability — also the most expensive, and needs an engineer

Within that last row, the frameworks split by philosophy as much as by feature: LangGraph favors explicit, auditable control, which matters when a workflow has compliance or review requirements; CrewAI favors fast prototyping, with agents playing defined roles on a team; AutoGen (Microsoft's AG2) favors agents that debate and refine each other's work. All three still need an engineer to stand up and maintain.

How to decide

1

Start with the agent mode already inside the tool you pay for. It's free, and it's often enough.

2

If your exact problem already has a vendor for it — enterprise search, CRM, IT tickets — buy that instead of building one.

3

If you want to wire together tools you already use, without writing code, a no-code builder is the middle ground.

4

Only build a custom framework when nothing off-the-shelf fits and you actually have engineering time to spend on it.

Watch-out

Don't skip straight to "we need an agent platform." Most of those conversations resolve once someone actually tries what the tool they already pay for can do on its own.

4 · Make It Habitual

This is the same loop as the rest of this site, pointed at your own AI use. Plan when you'll reach for it — fold it into a routine you already run, like weekly planning, meeting prep, or a first draft, instead of treating it as a novelty you open when you remember to. Do by actually using it on a real task, not a test question. Reflect on what it got right, what it got wrong, and update your own rule for when you trust it unchecked versus when you verify first.

Watch for both failure modes: letting the novelty wear off into disuse, and swinging the other way into pasting its output forward without reading it. The habit worth building isn't "use AI more" — it's "know which of today's tasks it should touch, and check the ones that matter."

Try It

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

Help me figure out whether a repetitive task I'm doing should move from one-off chats to something more automated. Ask me: what tool do I already use day to day, do I have engineering help available, does my problem already match a known category like IT support, customer service, or CRM, and how many times a week I actually do this task. Then recommend exactly one of: the agent mode built into my existing tool, a no-code builder, an off-the-shelf platform, or a custom build — and tell me why, in plain terms.
