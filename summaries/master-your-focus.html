Master Your Focus

A Practical Guide to Stop Chasing the Next Thing and Focus on What Matters Until It’s Done

by Thibaut Meurisse

2020 · 3h 58m audiobook · ★ 4.7 Audible

The big idea

Focus is a trainable skill, not a fixed trait. By deliberately managing attention across three levels, short-term, transitional, and long-term, you can stop chasing every new idea and actually finish what matters.

The three levels of focus
Short-term focus: staying on the task in front of you without distraction. Transitional focus: the routines and planning that carry momentum from one task to the next. Long-term focus: a clear, compelling vision that guides your daily decisions.

Most of the book's other techniques, prioritization, distraction management, goal-setting, map onto one of these three levels.

Key ideas

  1. Shiny object syndrome

    Naming the pattern of abandoning a project the moment a newer, more exciting opportunity appears, the core obstacle the book is written to solve.

  2. The cost of switching

    Cites research that it takes roughly 23 minutes to fully regain concentration after an interruption, making task-switching far more costly than it feels.

  3. Vision to daily task

    A cascading system: yearly goals to 90-day sprints to weekly targets to daily tasks, distinguishing process goals you control from result goals you don't.

  4. Ruthless prioritization

    Sorting tasks by urgent and important to decide what actually deserves focused attention versus what should be dropped or delegated.

  5. The single highest-leverage task

    Identify the one task that matters most and do it during your peak-energy hours, before anything else competes for attention.

  6. Root causes of procrastination

    Traces avoidance to unclear goals, unconfronted fears, and disconnection from a larger vision, and pairs each cause with a fix.

Try it this week

  1. Identify your single most important task for tomorrow and do it before checking phone or email.
  2. Sort this week's task list into urgent and important, and cut or delegate what's neither.
  3. Build a two-minute transition ritual, like a short walk or a few slow breaths, to use between tasks for one week.
  4. Try one distraction-free morning: no phone for the first hour after waking.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • People juggling too many projects or ideas at once
  • Goal-oriented readers who want a step-by-step system connecting vision to daily tasks

Less useful if

  • Lighter on the emotional or psychological root-cause work than more introspective books, more system than self-inquiry
  • Readers already familiar with the Eisenhower Matrix or SMART goals from other productivity books
