The Ride of a Lifetime

Lessons Learned from 15 Years as CEO of the Walt Disney Company

by Robert Iger

2019 · 8h 45m audiobook · ★ 4.8 Audible

The big idea

Iger's account of his 15 years as Disney CEO (2005-2020) argues that the tenure's defining moves -- acquiring Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, and 21st Century Fox, and launching Disney+ -- succeeded because of a consistent set of personal leadership behaviors rather than financial engineering alone.

The Ten Leadership Principles
In the closing chapter, Iger distills his tenure into ten named principles -- Optimism, Courage, Focus, Decisiveness, Curiosity, Fairness, Thoughtfulness, Authenticity, The Relentless Pursuit of Perfection, and Integrity. He illustrates optimism with his insistence Disney could out-innovate streaming disruption, and respect for the creative team with his approach to Pixar and Marvel: buying the companies but deliberately not imposing Disney's process on them. He ties the relentless pursuit of perfection to the years-long, largely secretive development of Disney+ technology.

The principles are retrospective and self-authored by the CEO whose tenure they describe, so they read as a flattering, coherent narrative applied after the fact -- published in 2019, the book predates later, more mixed outcomes like the Fox deal's subsequent write-downs.

Key ideas

  1. Buy the culture, don't merge it

    Iger's core M&A insight across Pixar, Marvel, and Lucasfilm was preserving each studio's independent creative culture rather than folding it into Disney's existing structure.

  2. The Pixar near-collapse story

    Iger describes personally repairing the fractured relationship between Michael Eisner's Disney and Steve Jobs's Pixar.

  3. Innovate or die on streaming

    Iger frames launching Disney+ and pulling content from Netflix as deliberately cannibalizing a profitable existing business before a competitor did it first.

  4. Succeeding Eisner

    Iger writes candidly about the boardroom conflict and political maneuvering behind his path to succeeding Michael Eisner as CEO.

  5. Crisis leadership case studies

    Chapters cover Iger's handling of operational crises as studies in decisiveness and communication.

  6. Succession as unfinished business

    Iger is candid that CEO succession planning was one of his hardest challenges, a theme that took on new resonance when he later returned as CEO in 2022.

Try it this week

  1. Before your next acquisition or team merger, decide explicitly what culture you will not try to change, and say so out loud to the team being acquired.
  2. Identify one profitable-but-declining part of your business and sketch what deliberately cannibalizing it now, before a competitor does, would look like.
  3. Write your own top five leadership principles in one page, each tied to one concrete decision where you actually applied it.
  4. Name your own succession plan's biggest open question and set a date to address it.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Senior leaders navigating large acquisitions or legacy-business disruption, especially in media and entertainment.
  • Aspiring executives wanting a first-person, CEO-level account of major deal-making and crisis management.

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting operational or tactical detail rather than narrative leadership reflection.
  • Readers seeking a critical outside perspective on Disney's labor practices or the more contested aspects of the Fox deal and later streaming losses.
