The Heart of Change

Real-Life Stories of How People Change Their Organizations

by John Kotter, Dan Cohen

2008 · 5h 33m audiobook · ★ 4.1 Audible

The big idea

People change their behavior less because they're given a compelling analysis and more because they're shown a vivid truth that changes how they feel, so successful change efforts have to appeal to emotion, not just logic.

See-Feel-Change
Instead of building the case for change through reports and data, Kotter and Cohen argue leaders should create a vivid, concrete experience that makes people feel the problem or opportunity directly, because emotion, not analysis, is what actually shifts behavior. Their signature example: rather than presenting a memo about wasteful purchasing, one leader piled 424 different glove types the company was separately buying onto a boardroom table, making the dysfunction impossible to ignore or rationalize away.

The book is a direct companion to Kotter's Leading Change, it retells the same eight-step model through real-company stories, so the two books cover the same structure with a different emphasis.

Key ideas

  1. Show, don't tell

    Concrete, visual, or experiential evidence of a problem moves people to act faster than a written analysis does.

  2. The eight steps, retold emotionally

    Each of Kotter's original eight change steps is reframed here around what people need to feel at each stage, not just do.

  3. Storytelling for buy-in

    Communicating a vision through stories that speak to people's anxieties and aspirations builds commitment more effectively than one-way announcements.

  4. Short-term wins build emotional momentum

    Visible early successes don't just prove the strategy works, they generate the confidence and energy needed to keep going.

  5. Removing blinders

    Leaders have to actively counter both false urgency and complacency, comfort that blocks any urgency at all.

  6. Culture change comes last, not first

    New behaviors have to visibly produce results before they get embedded into how we do things, so cultural change follows action rather than preceding it.

Try it this week

  1. Find one concrete, visual way to demonstrate a problem you're trying to fix instead of writing another memo about it.
  2. Draft the emotional core of your change story: what do people stand to feel, pride, relief, excitement, if the change succeeds?
  3. Identify a false-urgency or complacency blinder currently affecting your team, and name one action that would counter it.
  4. Plan how you'll visibly celebrate your next short-term win in a way people will feel, not just read about in a status update.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Leaders who have the analytical case for change but are struggling to get genuine buy-in
  • Anyone using Leading Change who wants real-company stories showing the emotional side of each step

Less useful if

  • Readers who want a purely conceptual or analytical model rather than illustrative case narratives
  • Those looking for new frameworks beyond Kotter's original eight steps, since this book largely retells rather than extends them
