How to Become CEO

The Rules for Rising to the Top of Any Organization

by Jeffrey J. Fox

2004 · 1h 36m audiobook · ★ 4.2 Audible

The big idea

Career advancement to the top of an organization is less about talent than about consistently applying a set of concrete, learnable behavioral rules -- from how you handle money conversations to how you treat receptionists -- that most people never think to treat as a deliberate strategy.

The 75 Rules (short-chapter commandments)
Fox structures the book as roughly 75 short, standalone rules covering compensation strategy (take the higher-paying job now, since future raises are usually a percentage of current salary), self-directed career management, customer orientation, and personal conduct (protect your reputation, write no angry memos, treat every person in the building with respect). The format is intentionally aphoristic -- each rule stands alone rather than building toward a unified model.

Several reviewers note the title oversells the content -- the rules are general professional-advancement wisdom rather than CEO-specific strategy.

Key ideas

  1. Take the higher salary now

    Because raises are usually set as a percentage of current pay, negotiating hard for starting salary has outsized long-term career leverage.

  2. Own your career plan

    No employer or HR department will proactively manage your advancement, so mapping your own path is the individual's job.

  3. Understand the customer, whatever your job

    Direct exposure to how customers buy and what they value reveals the real competitive picture faster than internal reporting.

  4. Reputation is built in small moments

    Rules about angry memos and treating junior staff with respect reflect the thesis that senior leaders are quietly evaluated on temperament long before results.

  5. Do the hard thing others avoid

    Deliberately taking on the unglamorous or difficult task nobody else wants is disproportionately remembered by decision-makers.

  6. Words are cheap, deeds are dear

    Fox repeatedly favors demonstrated action over stated intention -- promising less and delivering more is a career-defining habit.

Try it this week

  1. Before your next compensation conversation, calculate what a given starting number compounds to over five years of typical percentage raises.
  2. This week, identify one customer-facing reality about your company you don't currently understand firsthand, and go find out directly.
  3. Pick the one task on your team's backlog that everyone is avoiding, and take it.
  4. Audit your last five emails or messages sent in frustration and write a personal rule for a cooling-off period before you send the next one.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • An early-to-mid-career professional who wants short, concrete, easy-to-apply workplace conduct rules rather than a strategic framework.
  • Someone who responds well to aphoristic, list-based advice they can dip into randomly.

Less useful if

  • A senior executive or board-level reader looking for guidance specific to public-company governance or actual CEO succession politics.
  • A reader who wants an evidence-based model rather than personal maxims from consulting experience.
