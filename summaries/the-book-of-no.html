The Book of No

by Susan Newman

The big idea

Compulsive yes-saying, driven by guilt, fear of disappointing others, or a need for approval, leads to overcommitment, resentment, and burnout. Learning to say no clearly and without over-apologizing protects both your time and your relationships.

Buy time before you answer
When put on the spot, don't answer immediately. Ask for details about the request and use a holding response, such as 'let me check my schedule and get back to you,' before committing.

One of the book's recurring techniques across its Friends, Family, and Work sections for interrupting a reflexive yes.

Key ideas

  1. Yes to one thing is no to another

    Every commitment you accept displaces something else: time, energy, or a priority. Newman frames overcommitment as a hidden trade-off, not a free favor.

  2. Guilt is a wasted emotion

    Guilt over saying no rarely serves any real purpose and mainly drains mental energy. Naming it as wasted helps readers let go of it faster.

  3. No doesn't need an apology

    The book coaches plain refusals without over-explaining, since long justifications invite negotiation and more guilt.

  4. Scripts by relationship type

    Organized by friends, family, work colleagues, and difficult people, since the right way to decline a friend differs from declining a boss or a manipulative relative.

  5. The fallout is usually smaller than feared

    Others dwell on your refusal far less than you expect; the anticipated damage to a relationship is usually much smaller than imagined.

  6. Spot manipulation before you respond

    Recognizing pressure tactics and guilt trips, especially from family or pushy acquaintances, lets you respond deliberately instead of being maneuvered into agreement.

Try it this week

  1. Write a one-line no script in advance for a recurring request you always say yes to but resent.
  2. Next time you're asked for something on the spot, practice a buy-time response instead of answering immediately.
  3. Notice guilt after a refusal this week and consciously label it as wasted emotion.
  4. Say no once this week without adding an apology or long justification.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Chronic people-pleasers and yes-people
  • Anyone overwhelmed by family, social, or workplace obligations who wants quick, scenario-based scripts

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting an in-depth psychological framework rather than a large catalog of scripts
  • Anyone put off by examples that skew toward traditionally gendered caretaking roles
