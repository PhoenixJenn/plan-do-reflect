Management of Organizational Behavior

Utilizing Human Resources

by Paul Hersey, Kenneth Blanchard

8th ed., 2000 · first published 1969 · no modern consumer rating found

The big idea

There's no single best leadership style. The right one depends on how ready the follower is for the specific task in front of them — their competence and their commitment — not a fixed trait of the leader or a one-size-fits-all situation.

The four styles
Directing (S1): new to the task, tell them what to do. Coaching (S2): willing but still building skill, explain the why. Supporting (S3): skilled but unsure, share the decision. Delegating (S4): skilled and confident, hand off the outcome.

Plotted on two axes — how much Directive behavior, how much Supportive behavior — the four styles form a 2x2, not a ranked ladder.

Key ideas

  1. Readiness, not personality

    The model is about the follower's readiness for a specific task, not a label you pin on them permanently — the same person might be S1 on one task and S4 on another.

  2. Two independent behavior axes

    Directive behavior (structure, instruction) and Supportive behavior (encouragement, involvement) move independently — a leader can be high on both, low on both, or anywhere between.

  3. Match the style to the moment

    Over-directing a ready person reads as micromanagement; under-directing someone new reads as abandonment. The skill is matching, not defaulting.

  4. A planned decrease in supervision

    As readiness grows on a task, the leader should deliberately dial direction down and delegation up — a life-cycle, not a one-time call.

  5. Originally "Life Cycle Theory of Leadership"

    First published in 1969 under that name, later renamed Situational Leadership Theory — now in its 10th-plus edition.

Try it this week

  1. Pick one person on your team and one specific task — not the person in general.
  2. Rate their readiness (low, moderate, high) for just that task.
  3. Match your style to that rating, even if it's different from how you manage them on other tasks.
  4. Notice if you're over-directing someone who's actually ready for more autonomy, or the reverse.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • New managers learning to adapt style per person and per task, rather than applying one default approach to everyone
  • Anyone managing a team with a wide range of skill levels on the same work

Less useful if

  • You want current research — this is a foundational, decades-old management textbook; later editions update examples but the core model hasn't changed much
  • Looking for an emotional-intelligence angle — Primal Leadership covers that ground instead
