How to Talk to Anyone

92 Little Tricks for Big Success in Relationships

by Leil Lowndes

2015 · 8h 59m audiobook · ★ 4.2 Audible

The big idea

Social success comes less from raw charisma than from a set of specific, learnable verbal and nonverbal techniques that make other people feel noticed, valued, and comfortable in your presence.

The flooding smile
Instead of smiling reflexively the instant you meet someone, pause a beat, take in their face, then let a warm smile spread slowly across yours; the slight delay reads as genuine and personal rather than automatic.

One of Lowndes' opening first-impression moves, used alongside related nonverbal tricks like Sticky Eyes and the Big Baby Pivot.

Key ideas

  1. Sticky eyes

    Hold your gaze on the speaker a moment after they've finished talking, signaling full attention rather than a wandering mind.

  2. Big baby pivot

    Turn your entire body toward whoever is speaking to you, the way an infant fully turns toward a parent's voice.

  3. Whozit opener

    Break into a new group or approach a stranger by asking a low-risk question like who's that; it's an easy, natural entry point.

  4. Parroting

    When you're stuck for a response, repeat back the last few words someone said as a question, buying time while signaling you were listening.

  5. Grapevine glory

    Say something complimentary about someone to a third party, trusting it will circulate back; indirect praise often lands harder than direct praise.

  6. The munching mingler mistake

    Eat before networking events so your hands stay free and you're not stuck hovering near the buffet, avoiding the isolation trap.

Try it this week

  1. At your next introduction, deliberately delay your smile one beat before letting it spread.
  2. Practice sticky eyes in three conversations this week: hold eye contact a moment after the other person stops talking.
  3. Use the whozit opener at one social or work event to start a conversation with someone you don't know.
  4. Say something genuinely positive about a colleague to a third person and notice if it circulates back.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • People anxious about small talk or networking events
  • Readers who want concrete body-language scripts to rehearse

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting psychological theory or research backing
  • Anyone put off by techniques that can feel performative if used insincerely
