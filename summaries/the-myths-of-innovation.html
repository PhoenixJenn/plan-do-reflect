One-page summary · 2 min read

The Myths of Innovation

by Scott Berkun

2020 · 4h 50m audiobook · ★ 4.5 Audible

The big idea

Popular stories about how innovation happens, sudden epiphanies, lone geniuses, best-idea-always-wins, are dramatically oversimplified myths. Real innovation is messy, collaborative, incremental, and driven as much by persuasion, timing, and problem-framing as by raw idea quality.

The ten myths framework
Berkun structures the book around debunking ten specific myths, like the Myth of Epiphany and the Myth of the Lone Inventor, using each as a lens to show what actually drives successful innovation: deep immersion, incubation, collaboration, and champion-style persistence.

Not a single how-to formula but a myth-by-myth debunking structure; Berkun explicitly resists offering a universal innovation method, arguing no reliable innovation pipeline exists.

Key ideas

  1. Myth of epiphany

    Breakthrough ideas feel sudden but actually result from long periods of immersion and incubation, not a single flash of insight.

  2. Myth of the lone inventor

    Celebrated solo geniuses relied heavily on teams, predecessors, and existing ideas that history later erased from the story.

  3. Myth that the best idea wins

    Adoption depends on marketing, politics, timing, and ease of implementation, not merit alone.

  4. Ideas are not scarce, courage is

    Good ideas are common; what's rare is the willingness to champion an idea despite social resistance and risk of failure.

  5. Problem framing is the real work

    How a problem is defined largely determines the quality of the solution, so effective innovators spend disproportionate time on framing.

  6. Innovation isn't always good

    New innovations create winners and losers unpredictably, so progress should be evaluated, not assumed to be automatically positive.

Try it this week

  1. Pick one idea you've dismissed as impossible and spend 20 minutes reframing the problem statement instead of solving it.
  2. Write down the real, unglamorous origin story of one genius innovation you admire and note how many people it depended on.
  3. Before your next brainstorm, block out incubation time; step away from the problem for a day before deciding it's unsolvable.
  4. Practice pitching one of your ideas to a skeptical colleague, focusing on persuasion and timing rather than just explaining the idea's merits.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Innovation and product teams frustrated by genius-myth pressure
  • Managers wanting a realistic, historically grounded view of how change happens in organizations

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting a step-by-step innovation methodology or checklist
  • Those looking for motivational hero-narratives rather than myth-busting analysis
