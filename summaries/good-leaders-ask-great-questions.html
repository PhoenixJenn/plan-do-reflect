Good Leaders Ask Great Questions

Your Foundation for Successful Leadership

by John C. Maxwell

2014 · 8h 17m audiobook · ★ 4.6 Audible

The big idea

The single greatest lever for growth and leadership effectiveness is asking good questions, of yourself, your team, and mentors, because, as Maxwell puts it, you only get answers to the questions you actually ask.

The Five Whys
A questioning technique where a leader keeps asking why in response to each answer, roughly five times in a row, to move from a surface-level symptom to the underlying reasoning or motivation behind a problem or decision. Maxwell applies it both to diagnosing team or process problems and to leaders examining their own motives and blind spots.

The Five Whys technique predates this book, it originated in Toyota's production system; Maxwell adapts it for leadership self-examination and team problem-solving rather than originating it.

Key ideas

  1. Questions as Self-Education

    You only get answers to the questions you ask, so the quality of a leader's questions caps the quality of their own growth.

  2. Overcoming the Fear of Asking

    Leaders often avoid questions to appear competent, but asking demonstrates humility and confidence rather than weakness.

  3. Self-Directed Questions

    Leaders need to honestly interrogate their own blind spots, ego, and motives before they can credibly lead others.

  4. Spotting Future Leaders

    Maxwell frames traits like initiative, influence on peers, and follow-through as questions to ask when evaluating who's ready for more responsibility.

  5. Question Brainstorming Groups

    Regularly scheduled sessions where a team poses open questions about shared challenges instead of pushing pre-formed answers.

  6. A Listening Journal

    A habit of recording key insights and follow-up questions after significant conversations, to close the loop on what you're learning from others.

Try it this week

  1. In your next one-on-one, ask one open why or what question and let the silence sit instead of filling it with your own answer.
  2. Start a listening journal this week: after each significant conversation, jot down one insight and one follow-up question.
  3. Pick one recurring team problem and run the Five Whys on it in your next team meeting.
  4. Ask a trusted peer one question about your own blind spots or leadership weaknesses, and just listen to the answer without defending yourself.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Leaders who default to giving answers or directives rather than asking
  • Anyone preparing to do more mentoring, coaching, or one-on-one conversations

Less useful if

  • Readers looking for one overarching leadership theory rather than a question-by-question, almost FAQ-style structure
  • May feel repetitive alongside Maxwell's other list- or Q&A-style books
