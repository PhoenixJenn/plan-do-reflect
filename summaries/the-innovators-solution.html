The Innovator's Solution

Creating and Sustaining Successful Growth

by Clayton M. Christensen, Michael E. Raynor

2007 · 9h 21m audiobook · ★ 4.4 Audible

The big idea

Building on The Innovator's Dilemma, Christensen and Raynor shift from diagnosing why incumbents fail to prescribing how established companies can successfully create and sustain new-growth businesses through disruptive innovation.

Jobs to be done
Rather than segmenting customers by demographics, the authors argue companies should identify the specific job a customer is trying to get done in a given circumstance, then design a product that does that job better than the alternatives, including non-consumption. The book's signature example is a fast-food chain's milkshakes: data showed many were bought alone, in the morning, by commuters, so the milkshake's real job was to be an easy, long-lasting, one-handed companion for a boring commute, not a dessert, redirecting improvement efforts toward thickness and drink-through time rather than flavor variety.

Jobs to Be Done is closely associated with Christensen but was also developed and popularized in parallel by other practitioners, so attribution of the exact framework varies across sources.

Key ideas

  1. Jobs to be done

    Understanding the functional, emotional, and social job behind a purchase reveals opportunities that demographic segmentation misses entirely.

  2. Low-end disruption

    Targeting customers who are overserved by existing products with a simpler, cheaper offering that's good enough for their needs.

  3. New-market disruption

    Creating a product so much simpler or cheaper that people who previously couldn't participate in the market, non-consumers, now can.

  4. Resources, processes, and values

    An organization's capabilities and limits come from what it has, how it works, and what it prioritizes, and the same processes and values that enable core-business excellence often actively block disruptive initiatives.

  5. Good money vs. bad money

    Growth investments should be patient for growth, time to find the right strategy, but impatient for profit, a lean cost structure early, the reverse of how many companies fund new ventures.

  6. Emergent vs. deliberate strategy

    When a market is genuinely new, strategy should emerge from experimentation and learning rather than a fixed, upfront five-year plan.

Try it this week

  1. Pick one product you sell and write down the actual job customers are hiring it for, in their own words and circumstances.
  2. Look for a market segment your company treats as non-consumers and brainstorm a radically simpler offer that could reach them.
  3. Audit whether a current growth project is funded like a sustaining project when it should be patient for growth, impatient for profit instead.
  4. Identify one process or approval step from your core business that would slow or kill a genuinely new initiative, and ask if it needs its own separate path.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Senior leaders and innovation teams responsible for actually building new-growth businesses inside an established company
  • Product managers wanting a practical alternative to demographic segmentation

Less useful if

  • Readers who haven't read The Innovator's Dilemma and want the underlying disruption theory explained from scratch, since this book assumes it
  • Very early-stage solo founders, since much of the advice concerns organizational structure and capital allocation inside larger companies
