The Art of Innovation

Lessons in Creativity from IDEO, America's Leading Design Firm

by Tom Kelley

2016 · 8h 56m audiobook · ★ 4.3 Audible

The big idea

Innovation isn't a mysterious gift reserved for lone geniuses, it's a repeatable discipline built on close observation of real users, rapid low-fidelity prototyping, and a culture that gives small, autonomous teams room to fail quickly and often.

IDEO's brainstorming rules
Kelley lays out concrete ground rules IDEO uses to run productive brainstorms: sharpen the focus with a specific, customer-centered problem statement; establish playful rules posted on the wall, like defer judgment and encourage wild ideas; number ideas as they're generated to track volume; build on the ideas of others rather than just adding new ones; use visible surfaces like whiteboards and sticky notes so the group can see the whole idea space at once; and warm up with a quick unrelated exercise before diving into the real problem.

These are IDEO's own internal facilitation practices as described by Kelley, one firm's proven method rather than a universally validated science of brainstorming.

Key ideas

  1. Observation over focus groups

    IDEO designers watch real users in natural settings rather than relying on what people say they want in a focus group, surfacing ergonomic problems people couldn't articulate themselves.

  2. Designing with empathy

    Good design requires deliberately imagining the experience of users unlike the designer, including people with different abilities, habits, or contexts of use.

  3. Rapid, crude prototyping

    Encapsulated in the rule never go to a meeting without a prototype, IDEO treats fast, rough physical or paper models as a way to think and get feedback, prioritizing iteration speed over early polish.

  4. Hot teams

    High-performing project teams share traits, total dedication, tight deadlines, irreverence toward hierarchy, diverse disciplines in the room, and real autonomy over resources and decisions.

  5. Fail often to succeed sooner

    Early, cheap failures are a core input to the design process rather than something to be avoided, since each rough prototype that doesn't work narrows the path to one that does.

  6. Innovation as a systematized process

    Kelley frames IDEO's method as a repeatable sequence, understanding the market, observing real behavior, visualizing concepts, refining through iterative prototypes, and implementing, arguing this process can be taught, not just inherited as talent.

Try it this week

  1. Spend an hour directly observing real users or customers doing the task your product or service addresses, without interrupting to ask them questions.
  2. Run a 20-minute brainstorm on a current problem using explicit ground rules: defer judgment, go for quantity, and build on others' ideas.
  3. Build a rough, low-fidelity prototype of your next idea before your next planning meeting, instead of arriving with only a description.
  4. Pull together a small cross-functional team with a tight deadline and real decision-making autonomy for one upcoming initiative.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Product, service, and design teams looking to build a more user-centered innovation process
  • Leaders trying to build a culture that supports experimentation and fast iteration

Less useful if

  • Readers focused purely on strategy or finance rather than product and design process
  • Some references and tools feel dated since the book predates many current digital collaboration and prototyping tools
