Mindset

The New Psychology of Success

by Carol S. Dweck

2019 · 10h 23m audiobook · ★ 4.6 Audible

The big idea

People's core belief about whether abilities are fixed or can be developed, their mindset, shapes how they respond to challenge and failure. A growth mindset tends to produce more resilience, learning, and long-run achievement than a fixed mindset.

Fixed mindset vs. growth mindset
In a fixed mindset, people believe intelligence is a static trait, so they avoid challenges that risk exposing inadequacy and treat effort itself as a sign of low ability. In a growth mindset, people believe abilities can be developed through effort and better strategies, so setbacks become information rather than a verdict on their worth.

Dweck illustrates the framework across sports, business, such as Enron's talent-obsessed culture, and parenting, showing how praising ability versus effort shapes a child's mindset.

Key ideas

  1. Praise the process, not the person

    Praising ability tends to instill a fixed mindset in children, while praising effort and strategy supports a growth mindset.

  2. Failure as data, not identity

    In a growth mindset, a setback is information about what to try next, not evidence about your fundamental worth.

  3. The talent myth in organizations

    Companies that obsess over identifying and rewarding innate talent over developed skill can foster brittle cultures that hide problems instead of solving them.

  4. Mindset in relationships

    Fixed-mindset partners expect relationships to just work naturally if they're right for each other; growth-minded partners treat relationship skills as things to build together.

  5. Redefining success

    Fixed-mindset success is about proving yourself; growth-mindset success is about stretching your abilities and learning, regardless of the immediate outcome.

  6. Mindsets are learnable

    Mindset itself isn't fixed; people can shift from fixed to more growth-oriented thinking with awareness and practice.

Try it this week

  1. Catch yourself using fixed-mindset language this week, like I'm just not a math person, and reframe it as I haven't learned this yet.
  2. After your next setback, write down one concrete lesson or strategy adjustment instead of a judgment about your ability.
  3. If you mentor someone, practice praising their effort or strategy on a specific task rather than praising them as smart or talented.
  4. Pick one skill you've avoided because you weren't good at it and spend 20 minutes practicing it with a beginner's mindset.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Parents, teachers, and managers shaping how others respond to failure
  • Anyone wanting a lens for handling setbacks and building resilience

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting rigorous, unqualified science
  • Anyone expecting the dramatic real-world effect sizes often implied by popular coverage of the book
