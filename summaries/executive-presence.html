Executive Presence

The Missing Link Between Merit and Success

by Sylvia Ann Hewlett

2014 · 6h 17m audiobook · ★ 4.2 Audible

The big idea

Executive presence, not merit alone, is what gets people promoted into senior leadership, and it rests on three measurable pillars: gravitas, communication, and appearance.

The Three Pillars Framework (Gravitas / Communication / Appearance)
Hewlett's research-based model breaks executive presence into three weighted components: gravitas, roughly two-thirds of the perception, covering confidence, decisiveness, integrity, and grace under fire; communication, about a quarter, covering speaking skill, voice, and the ability to read a room; and appearance, a small remainder, polished, appropriate self-presentation. Leaders can audit and deliberately strengthen each pillar rather than treating presence as an innate trait.

The percentage weightings come from Hewlett's own executive surveys and shift slightly between the original book and the Executive Presence 2.0 edition.

Key ideas

  1. Gravitas is the entry ticket

    Composure under pressure, decisiveness, and integrity in high-stakes moments determine whether others trust a leader's judgment.

  2. Communication carries the message

    Voice, storytelling, eye contact, and reading the room determine whether gravitas even gets noticed.

  3. Appearance is a threshold, not a ceiling

    Looking the part removes a distraction, but polish can't substitute for substance.

  4. Double binds for women

    Assertiveness often reads as abrasive while accommodation reads as weak, narrowing the acceptable behavioral band.

  5. Sponsors over mentors

    Advancement depends less on private advice and more on advocates who speak up for you when you're not in the room.

  6. Presence is learnable

    Case examples show presence can be deliberately built through practice, not just inherited.

Try it this week

  1. Record yourself giving a 2-minute update, count filler words and rushed sentences, then re-record it cleaner.
  2. Ask a trusted colleague for one specific piece of presence feedback this week and visibly act on it.
  3. Before your next high-stakes meeting, write and rehearse a one-sentence point of view so you can state it without notes.
  4. Identify one senior person who could sponsor, not just mentor, you and ask for a specific, visible opportunity.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Mid-career professionals preparing for a promotion into senior leadership
  • Women and underrepresented professionals navigating bias in how presence is judged

Less useful if

  • Early-career individual contributors with little exposure to senior rooms yet
  • Readers wanting a psychological deep-dive rather than a practitioner framework built on survey data
