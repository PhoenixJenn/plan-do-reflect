Good Strategy/Bad Strategy

The Difference and Why It Matters

by Richard Rumelt

2019 · 11h 49m audiobook · ★ 4.6 Audible

The big idea

Most organizational strategy is actually fluff, goal-setting, or a list of aspirations dressed up as strategy; real strategy is a coherent response built on an honest diagnosis of the specific obstacle standing in the way.

The Kernel of Good Strategy
Rumelt argues every good strategy has the same three-part structure. First, a diagnosis that simplifies a complex situation down to the one or two critical factors actually determining outcomes -- Rumelt claims 80% of strategy work is in the diagnosis. Second, a guiding policy -- a general approach for dealing with the obstacle, like highway guardrails that channel action. Third, coherent actions -- coordinated, mutually reinforcing steps that carry out the guiding policy, rather than a wish list of disconnected initiatives.

The book is much stronger at diagnosing bad strategy than at teaching readers to construct good strategy from scratch -- it reads more like a rigorous critique toolkit than a step-by-step generative method.

Key ideas

  1. The four hallmarks of bad strategy

    Fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives that are either too vague or an unprioritized laundry list.

  2. Strategy as leverage

    Good strategy concentrates limited resources and attention at a pivot point where a small, well-aimed push produces disproportionate results.

  3. The importance of a proximate objective

    When a problem is too complex or uncertain, strong strategy narrows focus to an achievable near-term objective.

  4. Chain-link systems

    Some organizations' performance is governed by their weakest link, not their average performance.

  5. Using your advantage / exploiting inertia

    Good strategy often works by identifying where competitors are structurally slow or unable to respond.

  6. Strategic vs. administrative thinking

    Rumelt distinguishes strategy (choosing where and how to compete) from mere planning and budgeting.

Try it this week

  1. Write a one-paragraph diagnosis of the single biggest obstacle facing your team or business right now, without listing any solutions yet.
  2. Audit your last strategy document for fluff -- buzzwords or restated goals with no actual causal logic -- and cut anything that doesn't survive.
  3. Pick one current initiative and check whether its stated actions are actually coordinated with each other, or just a bundled wish list.
  4. Identify the weakest link constraining your team's output and redirect one resource allocation toward it instead of your strongest area.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Senior operators and executives who already write strategy documents but suspect their own strategy is really just a budget and aspirational goals.
  • Consultants and strategy leads who need a sharp, repeatable diagnostic lens for critiquing weak plans.

Less useful if

  • First-time managers with no existing strategy process to critique, who need step-by-step how-to guidance more.
  • Readers hoping for a workbook-style, fill-in-the-template approach.
