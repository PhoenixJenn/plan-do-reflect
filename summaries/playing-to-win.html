Playing to Win

How Strategy Really Works

by Roger L. Martin, A.G. Lafley

2013 · 7h 9m audiobook · ★ 4.4 Audible

The big idea

Strategy is not a vision statement or a list of values -- it is a small set of interlocking, hard choices, and the book distills this into a five-question strategy cascade built from Lafley's experience turning around Procter & Gamble as CEO.

The Strategic Choice Cascade
The cascade forces five sequential, interdependent choices: what is our winning aspiration; where will we play; how will we win in the chosen arena; what capabilities must be in place to win that way; and what management systems are needed to build and sustain those capabilities. Each answer constrains the next, and the authors insist the choices must be made together -- a where-to-play choice made without a clear how-to-win is just a target list, not a strategy.

Because the cascade is built almost entirely from Lafley's own P&G tenure, it's easier to validate with the benefit of hindsight; the book offers less guidance for situations where resource constraints limit how many of the five boxes a smaller company can realistically fill at once.

Key ideas

  1. Strategy is choice, not aspiration

    The authors explicitly reject vision/mission statements and SWOT-style lists as strategy, insisting real strategy commits to specific, exclusionary choices.

  2. Reverse-engineering where to play, how to win

    Rather than starting from capabilities and asking what's possible, the method starts from winning and works backward to what must be true.

  3. Competitive advantage as the test

    Every how-to-win choice must be checked against what makes the company distinctively better for its chosen customer, not just adequate.

  4. What would have to be true

    The book's companion methodology asks readers to state the conditions that would have to hold for a strategic option to succeed.

  5. Capabilities must match the chosen how

    A recurring failure mode is companies picking a competitive advantage their existing capabilities can't actually deliver.

  6. Management systems as the hidden strategy layer

    Metrics, incentives, and governance are treated as part of strategy itself, not an afterthought to implementation.

Try it this week

  1. Write your team's current winning aspiration in one sentence specific enough that it rules out some of what you're currently doing.
  2. List three markets or segments your team should explicitly stop investing in as part of a where-to-play choice.
  3. State your how-to-win claim and list the two or three capabilities that would have to be true for it to hold.
  4. Run the what-would-have-to-be-true test on one current strategic bet under debate on your team.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • General managers and executives who need a practical, choice-forcing framework rather than an academic theory of strategy.
  • Teams stuck producing vision statements and SWOT lists instead of decisions.

Less useful if

  • Readers in fast-moving startups or emergent markets where where-to-play and how-to-win shift too quickly for a cascade built on a large, slow-moving company.
  • Anyone looking for rigorous outside evaluation of whether the framework caused P&G's results.
