Move Your Bus

An Extraordinary New Approach to Accelerating Success in Work and Life

by Ron Clark

2015 · 4h 1m audiobook · ★ 4.8 Audible

The big idea

An organization only moves forward when its people actively push it forward. Success depends less on the driver, the leader, alone than on getting the largest possible share of passengers to contribute effort, since even one dead-weight rider slows the whole bus.

The bus passenger types
Clark sorts every team member into one of five roles: Runners, high-effort top performers; Joggers, steady but lacking confidence to push harder; Walkers, minimal, resistant-to-change contributors; Riders, dead weight who only perform when watched; and the Driver, the leader who steers.

Clark's advice differs by type: nurture and reward Runners, build Joggers' confidence, and address Walkers and Riders directly rather than ignore them.

Key ideas

  1. Runners drive momentum

    Top performers need visible recognition and room to innovate, not micromanagement, or they disengage.

  2. Joggers need confidence, not pressure

    Steady performers often have more capacity than they show; encouragement moves them toward Runner behavior.

  3. Riders slow everyone down

    Non-performers who coast unnoticed create resentment among high performers and must be addressed directly.

  4. Set explicit, high expectations

    Clear, specific, achievable goals, communicated openly, are more motivating than vague encouragement.

  5. Accountability without entitlement

    Nobody is owed recognition or reward; in high-performing cultures, contribution has to be earned continually.

  6. Individualized management

    Different passenger types require different leadership approaches; a one-size-fits-all style under-serves both top and struggling performers.

Try it this week

  1. Privately sort your team into Runner, Jogger, Walker, or Rider, and notice where your own energy goes.
  2. Give one specific, public acknowledgment to a Runner this week instead of a generic compliment.
  3. Have one direct, non-confrontational conversation with a Rider or Walker about a concrete expectation.
  4. Audit one recurring commitment you've been coasting on and decide whether to re-engage as a Runner or hand it off.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Team leaders and managers
  • People frustrated by uneven effort on their team

Less useful if

  • Individual contributors with no direct reports
  • Readers wanting nuanced HR or ethical guidance on managing out underperformers
