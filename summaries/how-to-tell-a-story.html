How to Tell a Story

The Essential Guide to Memorable Storytelling from The Moth

by The Moth, Meg Bowles, Catherine Burns

2022 · 10h 8m audiobook · ★ 4.2 Audible

The big idea

True, first-person personal stories, told aloud, not read, build genuine connection when they have real stakes and show how the teller changed. The Moth distills over 25 years of coaching storytellers into a repeatable process for finding and shaping those stories.

Narrative stepping stones
Instead of narrating everything chronologically, identify the handful of key moments, scenes, brief summarizing bridges, and reflections, that the audience actually needs to follow the arc, and build the story around just those.

The Moth's own coaching technique, developed for their StorySLAM and Moth Radio Hour storytellers and explained by longtime Moth directors and editors.

Key ideas

  1. Story seeds

    Mine your memory for specific moments that taught you something or marked a turning point in who you were becoming.

  2. Stakes and change

    Every Moth-worthy story needs what you wanted or feared, what you gained or lost, and how you were different afterward.

  3. True, first-person, spoken

    The Moth's core rule: no notes, no fiction, no reading from a page; the story must be told live, in your own words.

  4. Launching and landing

    Craft a strong first line to orient the audience and a strong last line that gives the story a satisfying close.

  5. Familiarization, not memorization

    Internalize the shape and key beats of the story through repeated telling rather than memorizing a script word for word.

  6. Test with a trusted audience

    Try the story out loud on a friend before the real telling to check pacing, clarity, and emotional impact.

Try it this week

  1. Write down three story seeds: moments when you learned something about yourself.
  2. Pick one and outline it as 4 to 5 stepping stones instead of a full script.
  3. Tell it out loud to someone, with no notes, aiming for under 5 minutes.
  4. Draft your opening line and closing line first, then build the middle to connect them.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Aspiring public speakers and personal storytellers
  • People preparing toasts, eulogies, or interview stories

Less useful if

  • Not a business-pitch or slide-deck presentation guide
  • Less relevant for data-heavy or technical talks
