It Worked for Me

In Life and Leadership

by Colin Powell

The big idea

Effective leadership isn't a grand theory -- it's built from small, repeatable habits of character (optimism, ownership, attention to detail, treating people well) that Colin Powell distilled over a military and diplomatic career spanning four presidential administrations, illustrated through 45 short personal stories rather than a single continuous argument.

Powell's Thirteen Rules of Leadership
The Thirteen Rules are leadership aphorisms Powell developed earlier in his career and revisits throughout the book, each expanded into a short chapter built around a personal story. They range from mindset rules like 'It ain't as bad as you think -- it will look better in the morning' and 'Perpetual optimism is a force multiplier,' to accountability rules like owning decisions rather than deferring blame, to practical operating rules like sweating the details and being generous with credit.

The rules are memorable aphorisms rather than an analytical framework -- they don't decompose into steps or diagnostics, so readers looking for a rigorous leadership model may find them thin on their own without the surrounding anecdotes.

Key ideas

  1. Optimism as a force multiplier

    Powell treats visible, communicated optimism from a leader as an operational asset that changes how hard a team fights through setbacks.

  2. Own your call, publicly

    Several stories center on Powell taking sole responsibility for decisions that went badly rather than distributing blame across the chain of command.

  3. Detail work is leadership work

    Powell repeatedly credits mundane attentiveness -- knowing logistics, schedules, and small facts cold -- as what let him make faster, better high-level judgment calls.

  4. The U.N. speech as a case study in institutional trust

    Powell directly revisits his 2003 U.N. presentation on Iraqi weapons of mass destruction, calling the episode 'a blot' on his record.

  5. Anger has a shelf life

    One rule explicitly instructs getting angry, then deliberately moving past it, treating prolonged anger as a leadership liability.

  6. Separate ego from position

    Powell warns that tying self-worth to title or rank makes a leader fragile and defensive exactly when they most need to accept hard feedback.

Try it this week

  1. The next time a decision you made goes wrong, state ownership of it directly to your team before explaining context or mitigating factors.
  2. Pick one recurring meeting and prepare with one more layer of logistical detail than you normally would, and notice whether it changes the discussion.
  3. After your next frustrating conflict at work, set a personal time limit for how long you'll let it affect your mood or decisions.
  4. In your next team win, write down every person who contributed before you write down what you personally did.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Early- to mid-career managers who want an accessible, story-driven introduction to leadership character rather than a management textbook.
  • Readers interested in Powell's own reflections on accountability and institutional trust, including his direct account of the 2003 U.N. Iraq speech.

Less useful if

  • Readers seeking a structured, research-backed leadership methodology -- the book is anecdotal and aphoristic rather than analytical.
  • Readers only interested in strict military strategy or foreign-policy history.
