Emotional Intelligence Habits

by Travis Bradberry

2023 · 15h 11m audiobook · ★ 4.6 Audible

The big idea

Building on Bradberry's earlier EQ 2.0 framework, this book argues that high-EQ people share a specific set of daily habits, not just insight, across sleep, self-awareness, body language, conflict, and life balance, and that these habits, more than raw intelligence, predict success.

The Habit-Stacking Approach to EQ (Emotion, Impulse, and Social Journals)
Rather than a single test-and-strategy cycle, the book recommends keeping short daily journals to build EQ habits: an emotion journal (trigger, feeling, reaction), an impulse journal (impulsive decisions and their triggers, used to practice delaying gratification), and a social journal (interactions and the nonverbal cues you noticed in others). The idea is that writing these down repeatedly turns one-off insight into an automatic habit.

This is a synthesis drawn from third-party chapter summaries of the book rather than a single named framework the publisher promotes as a trademarked tool, so treat the journal names as descriptive rather than the author's exact terminology.

Key ideas

  1. Sleep as an EQ foundation

    The book treats consistent sleep (regular wake times, cutting afternoon caffeine, limiting blue light, cooling the bedroom) as a prerequisite for emotional regulation, not a separate wellness topic.

  2. Self-awareness is real-time, not retrospective

    The habit is catching your emotional state as it's happening, not just reflecting on it afterward.

  3. Body language is a two-way skill

    High-EQ people both read others' nonverbal cues and consciously manage their own, since nonverbal signals shape how colleagues perceive competence.

  4. Conflict tolerance correlates with advancement

    The book cites research linking how gracefully someone handles workplace conflict to career progression.

  5. Life balance is framed as a performance lever, not a trade-off

    The claim is that integrating renewal and relationships into a demanding career improves performance rather than diluting it.

  6. Emotionally intelligent people still feel friction

    The book is explicit that high EQ doesn't mean an absence of stress or conflict, it means handling the same friction with more poise.

Try it this week

  1. Pick one weeknight this week to set a fixed wind-down time and cut caffeine after noon, then note the next morning whether your emotional reactivity feels different.
  2. For three days, keep a one-line impulse journal, write down any decision made in the heat of the moment and what triggered it.
  3. In your next disagreement at work, consciously pause for three seconds before responding, and note afterward whether that changed the outcome.
  4. Send one specific, personal appreciation note to a colleague or direct report this week instead of a generic thanks.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Readers who already know the basic EQ framework and want daily-habit mechanics
  • People who like journaling-based self-improvement systems

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting a first introduction to emotional intelligence theory
  • Anyone looking for a rigorously peer-reviewed research base rather than a practitioner-style pop-business synthesis
