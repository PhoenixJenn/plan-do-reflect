The big idea

This Harvard Business Review anthology argues that strategy is not a single technique but a discipline with several distinct, sometimes competing schools -- positioning (Porter), value innovation (Kim/Mauborgne), business-model design (Johnson/Christensen), and execution discipline -- and compiles the articles considered most foundational to each.

Porter's Five Forces / Activity-Fit Framework (from the featured article 'What Is Strategy?')
The anchor article argues operational effectiveness is not strategy because competitors can copy it; real strategy means choosing a distinct position and making explicit trade-offs -- deliberately choosing not to do certain things. Porter shows this with Southwest Airlines, which forgoes meals and seat assignments to sustain a uniquely low-cost position. The framework's deeper claim is about fit: competitive advantage comes from activities reinforcing one another, which is why rivals struggle to imitate a whole system even when they can copy individual pieces.

Because this is a curated anthology spanning multiple authors and decades, the tools inside genuinely conflict with each other in places -- Porter's trade-off-driven positioning sits uneasily next to Kim/Mauborgne's value-innovation logic.

Key ideas

  1. Strategy vs. operational effectiveness

    Porter's opening distinction -- that doing existing activities better is not the same as choosing a different, defensible position -- sets the frame for the collection.

  2. Business model reinvention

    Johnson, Christensen, and Kagermann's article breaks a business model into four interlocking parts as a diagnostic for whether a new opportunity needs a genuinely new model.

  3. Blue ocean value curves

    The Kim/Mauborgne article condenses their book's strategy-canvas logic into a standalone piece.

  4. Building a durable vision

    Collins and Porras separate a company's timeless core ideology from its changeable strategies and practices.

  5. The balanced scorecard as a management system

    Kaplan and Norton's article extends their scorecard into a full system for translating strategy into operational targets.

  6. Who has the D (decision rights)

    Rogers and Blenko argue execution fails most often from unclear decision rights, offering the RAPID framework for assigning them.

Try it this week

  1. Read the Porter and Kim/Mauborgne articles back to back and write down where their advice would actually conflict for your specific business.
  2. Apply the four-part business model framework to a new initiative your team is considering.
  3. Identify one recurring decision where it's genuinely unclear who has final authority, and propose a RAPID-style assignment for it.
  4. Separate your team's core purpose from its current tactics using the Collins/Porras lens, and check whether recent changes have blurred the two.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • MBA students, consultants, or managers who want a single-volume survey of the most-cited strategy frameworks.
  • Executives building an internal strategy curriculum who need canonical, HBR-vetted source material to cite.

Less useful if

  • Readers who want one coherent, end-to-end methodology -- the anthology format means switching frameworks and voices every 20-30 pages.
  • Anyone who has already read the original full-length books several of these articles are condensed from.
