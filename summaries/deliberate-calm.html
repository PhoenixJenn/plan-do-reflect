Deliberate Calm

How to Learn and Lead in a Volatile World

by Jacqueline Brassey, Aaron De Smet, Michiel Kruyt

2022 · 9h 10m audiobook · ★ 4.5 Audible

The big idea

Leaders can deliberately build the capacity to stay calm and think clearly amid volatility by developing dual awareness of their inner and outer world, rather than simply trying to push through pressure with willpower.

Dual Awareness Practice
Dual awareness means simultaneously tracking two things in a high-stakes moment: your external environment and your internal state, the emotions, assumptions, and bodily reactions driving your response. The authors argue most people default into a fixed habitual zone of reaction under stress; dual awareness creates a pause between trigger and response, opening space to shift into a more flexible adaptive zone and choose a deliberate reaction instead of a reflexive one.

One secondary summary cites a statistic that executives trained in this approach performed dramatically better under stress than a control group; this figure comes from a single secondary source and could not be independently corroborated, so treat it cautiously rather than as a verified research claim.

Key ideas

  1. Habitual Zone vs. Adaptive Zone

    Under pressure, people tend to fall back on fixed, familiar patterns; deliberate calm is about consciously shifting into a more flexible, experimental mode.

  2. The Iceberg Principle

    Visible behavior is only about 10% of the picture, the other 90% is made up of hidden beliefs, assumptions, and mental models that actually drive how someone reacts.

  3. Four Supporting Mindsets

    A growth mindset, a curious mindset, a creative mindset, and an agent mindset, believing you can influence outcomes.

  4. Learning Agility

    Treating stressful, high-stakes moments as data to extract lessons from afterward, not just events to survive in the moment.

  5. A Four-Week Self-Governance Protocol

    A staged practice, first identifying emotional triggers, then building baseline awareness, then adding calming practices, then actively replacing self-defeating patterns.

  6. The Sully Sullenberger Case

    The emergency Hudson River landing is used as the book's central illustration of composure and clear decision-making under extreme, sudden pressure.

Try it this week

  1. For one week, log four moments a day when you felt emotionally triggered, noting what set it off and how you responded.
  2. Before reacting to stressful news this week, pause and name, even silently, what's happening in your body and mind before you respond.
  3. Open one meeting this week with 60 seconds of deep breathing or intention-setting before diving into the agenda.
  4. Pick one recurring stress trigger, write down the assumption driving your usual reaction to it, and test an alternative interpretation next time it happens.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Leaders navigating sustained uncertainty or crisis
  • People who want a research-framed approach to emotional regulation at work

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting quick tactical tips rather than a multi-week self-development program
  • Those already well-versed in mindfulness or somatic practices may find the core ideas familiar under new labels
