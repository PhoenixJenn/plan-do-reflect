Think Again

The Power of Knowing What You Don't Know

by Adam Grant

2021 · 6h 40m audiobook · ★ 4.7 Audible

The big idea

In a fast-changing world, the ability to doubt what you know, question your opinions, and update your beliefs matters more than raw intelligence or conviction. Grant argues most people default to defending their existing views rather than rethinking them.

The scientist mindset
Instead of arguing like a preacher defending sacred beliefs, a prosecutor attacking others' views to win, or a politician telling people what they want to hear, think like a scientist: treat your opinions as hypotheses to test, actively seek disconfirming evidence, and update your views when the evidence warrants it.

Grant pairs this with confident humility, staying confident in your ability to figure things out while staying humble about your current conclusions, and extends the idea to persuasion via motivational-interviewing-style questioning.

Key ideas

  1. Preacher, prosecutor, politician, scientist

    Four mental modes people slip into when their beliefs are challenged; only the scientist mode treats a belief as testable rather than something to defend, attack, or spin.

  2. Confirmation and desirability bias

    We tend to see what we expect to see and what we want to see, which locks us into an overconfidence cycle that resists updating.

  3. Confident humility

    Real confidence pairs trust in your ability to grow with humility about your present knowledge, the opposite of both arrogance and self-doubt paralysis.

  4. Task conflict vs. relationship conflict

    Productive disagreement over ideas improves decisions; personal conflict shuts down rethinking, so good teams cultivate the former and defuse the latter.

  5. Motivational interviewing for persuasion

    Asking open-ended questions and letting people articulate their own reasons for change is more effective at shifting minds than presenting facts.

  6. Psychological safety enables rethinking

    Groups and organizations rethink and learn faster when people feel safe admitting uncertainty or being wrong without fear of punishment.

Try it this week

  1. Pick one strongly held opinion and write down what evidence would actually change your mind about it.
  2. In your next disagreement, ask two open-ended questions before stating your own position.
  3. Identify one belief you hold mainly because of where or how you grew up, and consider whether it still holds up on its own merits.
  4. Practice saying I don't know or I changed my mind out loud once this week when it's true.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • People in leadership, negotiation, or persuasion-heavy roles
  • Anyone wanting to reduce overconfidence and improve decision-making

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting quick tactical productivity tips rather than a mindset deep-dive
  • Those already well versed in cognitive bias literature, who may find some ground familiar
