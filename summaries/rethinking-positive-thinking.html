Rethinking Positive Thinking

by Gabriele Oettingen

The big idea

Pure positive fantasizing about a desired future can backfire: it gives the brain a false sense of already having succeeded, which saps the energy needed to actually pursue the goal. Oettingen's research shows that pairing optimism with a clear-eyed look at real obstacles is what actually drives motivation and achievement.

WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan
A four-step exercise: name a feasible Wish, vividly imagine the best Outcome, identify the real internal Obstacle standing in your way, then make a concrete if-then Plan for how you'll respond when that obstacle shows up.

WOOP is Oettingen's own research-backed tool, built from decades of her published mental contrasting studies rather than folk psychology.

Key ideas

  1. Dwelling vs. mental contrasting

    Fantasizing about success alone relaxes the body and mind as if the goal were already reached, while contrasting that fantasy with present obstacles creates productive tension that fuels action.

  2. Positive fantasies can backfire

    In her studies, women who vividly visualized weight loss lost fewer pounds, and job seekers who daydreamed about ideal jobs sent fewer applications and earned less.

  3. A critique of the cult of optimism

    The book directly pushes back on parts of the positive-psychology movement that treat unconditional optimism as automatically good for motivation.

  4. Obstacles should be internal, not external

    WOOP works best when the identified obstacle is something within you, a habit, emotion, or thought pattern, rather than an external circumstance you can't control.

  5. If-then planning links mind and behavior

    Pre-deciding if obstacle X happens, then I will do Y lets the brain automatically trigger the planned response.

  6. Grounded, not anti-optimism

    The method isn't about pessimism; it still starts from a genuine, felt wish and outcome, it just refuses to let fantasy substitute for a plan.

Try it this week

  1. Pick one real wish for this week and run it through WOOP on paper: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan, in that order.
  2. Before visualizing a goal, pause and ask what inside me is most likely to get in the way, instead of only picturing success.
  3. Write one if-then plan today, like if I feel too tired to write after dinner, then I'll write for just 5 minutes at my desk.
  4. Notice one moment this week when you catch yourself dwelling, enjoying the fantasy of a finished goal, and consciously contrast it with the next obstacle you'll actually face.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Goal-setters who feel stuck despite a positive attitude
  • Readers who want evidence-based psychology rather than pure motivational rhetoric

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting a quick, breezy read; the discovery process could be more concise
  • Those looking for pure inspiration rather than research and methodology
