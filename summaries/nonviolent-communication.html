Nonviolent Communication

Create Your Life, Your Relationships, and Your World in Harmony with Your Values

by Marshall Rosenberg

2015 · 5h 9m audiobook · ★ 4.8 Audible

The big idea

Most conflict and disconnection comes from a habitual, judgment-based way of speaking and listening. By observing without evaluating, naming genuine feelings, connecting them to underlying needs, and making clear requests, not demands, people can communicate in a way that stays connected to compassion even in conflict.

The four-part NVC process, OFNR
State a concrete Observation, a fact, not a judgment, name the Feeling it brings up, identify the underlying Need or value driving that feeling, and make a specific, actionable Request, not a demand. For example: when I see or hear X, I feel Y, because I need Z; would you be willing to do W?

Formalized by Rosenberg as the 4-Part NVC Process, taught today by the Center for Nonviolent Communication exactly as OFNR.

Key ideas

  1. Observation vs. evaluation

    Separate what actually happened, a fact a camera could record, from your interpretation of it; mixing the two triggers defensiveness in the listener.

  2. Feelings vs. thoughts

    True feeling words, hurt, anxious, relieved, differ from disguised judgments, like feeling ignored or attacked, which are actually interpretations of the other person's behavior.

  3. Underlying needs

    Every feeling points to a met or unmet universal human need, connection, autonomy, safety; naming the need, not the other person's fault, is the path to resolution.

  4. Requests vs. demands

    A request stays a request only if you can accept no gracefully; if refusal brings punishment or guilt-tripping, it was really a demand.

  5. Jackal vs. giraffe language

    Rosenberg used jackal to represent judgmental, blaming speech and giraffe to represent compassionate NVC-style speech, as a teaching metaphor.

  6. Self-empathy and anger

    Anger is treated as a signal of an unmet need rather than something to suppress or vent destructively; process it by identifying the need beneath it before speaking.

Try it this week

  1. Rewrite one recent complaint using OFNR: state the observable fact, your feeling, your need, and a concrete request, before you say it out loud.
  2. Catch yourself using a judgment word this week and translate it into an observation plus a feeling instead.
  3. Practice empathic listening once: when someone vents to you, reflect back their feelings and needs instead of offering advice or defending yourself.
  4. Next time you're angry, pause and ask what need of mine isn't being met right now before responding.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • People wanting to reduce blame and defensiveness in close relationships
  • Therapists, mediators, parents, and communication-skills learners

Less useful if

  • Readers in acute, high-stakes adversarial negotiations needing fast tactics
  • Those who find the required language patterns stilted or slow-going at first
