Conflict at Work

A Toolkit for Managing Your Emotions for Successful Results.

by M Paula Daoust

2021 · 6h 2m audiobook · ★ 4.8 Audible

The big idea

Workplace conflict is fundamentally an emotional event, not a rational one, which is why standard, purely logic-based conflict-resolution training often fails. Daoust offers a flexible toolkit of techniques rather than one rigid method, so readers can pick what fits their own style.

The Aiki approach
Named for the aikido principle of blending with an opponent's energy rather than meeting it head-on, this technique is framed as a way to defuse a conflict by working with the other person's emotional energy instead of resisting or escalating it.

The Aiki tool and an accompanying worksheet are confirmed via the author's own site; the detailed step-by-step mechanics couldn't be independently verified beyond that, so treat specifics beyond blend-don't-resist as unconfirmed.

Key ideas

  1. Conflict is emotional first

    Daoust's core argument is that people mismanage conflict because they treat it as a logic problem when it's really an emotional one.

  2. Two bad defaults

    Most people oscillate between two ineffective patterns: demanding what they want and damaging the relationship, or avoiding conflict and giving up what they need.

  3. A toolkit, not one method

    Rather than a single framework, the book offers multiple selectable tools so readers can combine strategies that suit their personality.

  4. Irrational beliefs fuel escalation

    Companion material addresses distorted thinking patterns that intensify workplace conflict.

  5. Forgiveness as a conflict tool

    A dedicated resource in the toolkit treats forgiveness as a practical step for moving past a conflict, not just a moral nicety.

  6. Built for the real workplace

    Daoust illustrates concepts through workplace scenarios rather than abstract theory.

Try it this week

  1. Before your next tense work conversation, name the emotion you're feeling, not just the issue, before you say anything.
  2. Identify one recurring work conflict where you default to either over-demanding or avoiding, and write down what a middle path would look like.
  3. Notice one irrational or catastrophizing thought during a conflict this week and write a more balanced version of it.
  4. After a disagreement is resolved, deliberately practice a small act of letting go rather than replaying the grievance.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • People whose conflict-resolution training hasn't stuck because it was too rational or scripted
  • Managers wanting emotional self-regulation tools for workplace friction

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting a single, clearly defined step-by-step model, since this is explicitly a pick-what-fits toolkit
  • Anyone needing frameworks for large organizational disputes rather than individual emotional regulation
