The Art of Public Speaking

Lessons from the Greatest Speeches in History

by John R. Hale, The Great Courses

2013 · 6h 15m audiobook · ★ 4.3 Audible

The big idea

Public speaking is a learnable craft, not an innate talent. Hale argues that studying how great historical orators, from Demosthenes to MLK, handled preparation, structure, and audience connection gives modern speakers a time-tested toolkit.

The power of three
Structuring key points, examples, or arguments in groups of three, illustrated via classic speeches, as a memorable, rhythmically persuasive way to organize a speech's content.

One of several structural devices taught across the course's 12 lectures, alongside narrative organization, logical sequencing, and vivid imagery.

Key ideas

  1. Speaking is learnable

    The central premise is that confidence and skill come from practice and known techniques, not natural gift, illustrated by Demosthenes, who reportedly overcame a speech impediment through relentless practice.

  2. Delivery matters as much as words

    Voice control, pitch variation, posture, and body language are taught as core delivery skills.

  3. Personal connection sells the message

    Sharing genuine experience and vulnerability is framed as a way speakers build trust with an audience.

  4. Humor as a tool, not a distraction

    Well-placed humor reinforces rather than undercuts a speech's main point.

  5. Tailoring tone to the audience

    Demonstrates adapting delivery and message to different audiences.

  6. Ending with a call to action

    Illustrates the importance of a clear, memorable close that moves listeners to act.

Try it this week

  1. Rewrite one key section of an upcoming talk or email using a rule-of-three structure for your main points.
  2. Practice a two-minute personal story you could use to open your next presentation, focused on a real, specific experience.
  3. Record yourself giving a short speech and review only your vocal pitch and pacing, not your content.
  4. Draft a closing line for your next presentation that gives listeners one explicit action to take.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Public-speaking beginners or infrequent presenters
  • History and rhetoric enthusiasts who like learning through famous speeches

Less useful if

  • Experienced or professional speakers; some reviewers found it very basic
  • Listeners wanting actual audio clips of the speeches discussed, which the course reportedly doesn't include
