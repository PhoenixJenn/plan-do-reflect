The Art of Strategic Communication

A Police Chief’s Guide to Mastering Soundbites, Storytelling, and Community Engagement

by Christopher Cook

2024 · 7h 43m audiobook · ★ 4.7 Audible

The big idea

Police Chief Christopher Cook argues that law enforcement leaders should treat the media as a bridge to public trust rather than an adversary, and that fast, transparent, proactive communication, especially around critical incidents, builds more community trust than silence or delay.

Transparency-first crisis response
Release accurate information quickly after critical incidents, including body-cam or video release, rather than waiting for a perfect statement, on the logic that bad news does not get better because you stick your head in the sand.

Grew out of Cook's real-world experience, including his 2020 congressional testimony after George Floyd's death, after which the DOJ asked him to build law-enforcement communication training now used across Texas.

Key ideas

  1. Media as bridge, not enemy

    Reframes the press and public as partners in building trust rather than obstacles to manage.

  2. Humanize the department

    Regularly sharing officers' everyday positive community interactions counters a purely incident-driven public image.

  3. Own your mistakes

    Publicly acknowledging errors builds more long-term trust than defensiveness.

  4. Multi-channel engagement

    Reach community members through social media, podcasts, mobile apps, digital dashboards, and town halls, not just press releases.

  5. Soundbite discipline

    Prepare tight, quotable, jargon-free statements in advance so spokespeople aren't improvising under pressure.

  6. Soft-skill fundamentals

    Covers practical basics too, email etiquette, handling difficult conversations, and conduct at press conferences.

Try it this week

  1. Draft a one-paragraph critical incident holding statement template your team could adapt within the first hour of an event.
  2. Identify one channel your organization isn't using yet and sketch a single post or episode.
  3. Write three jargon-free soundbites for a topic you often get asked about.
  4. Review a past difficult announcement and rewrite it applying share the facts fast instead of wait until certain.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Police chiefs and public information officers
  • Public-sector communicators handling crisis or community relations

Less useful if

  • General or corporate communicators outside public safety
  • Readers wanting communication theory rather than field-tested law-enforcement practice
