Where Good Ideas Come From

The Natural History of Innovation

by Steven Johnson

2010 · 7h 10m audiobook · ★ 4.3 Audible

The big idea

Innovation is not a single aha moment but the predictable product of certain environments and conditions, networked, error-tolerant, recombinant systems, that recur again and again in nature, cities, and technology.

The adjacent possible
The adjacent possible is the set of ideas, inventions, and combinations that are one step reachable from what already exists at a given moment, given current knowledge, materials, and tools. Johnson argues breakthrough innovation happens by combining existing elements just past the current boundary, not by leaping far beyond it. His example: Charles Babbage's 1830s mechanical computer concept was outside the adjacent possible of his time because the necessary electronics didn't exist yet; a century later, once surrounding technology caught up, the same basic blueprint became buildable.

Key ideas

  1. Liquid networks

    Innovation flourishes in dense, loosely connected environments, like cities or coffeehouses, where ideas move quickly between people, rather than in isolated, rigid hierarchies.

  2. Slow hunches

    Big ideas are usually not sudden insights but incomplete hunches that need months or years, and often collision with someone else's hunch, before they mature into something usable.

  3. Exaptation

    Traits or technologies developed for one purpose often get repurposed for an entirely different, more consequential use, a pattern that recurs throughout technology history.

  4. Platforms

    New innovations tend to stack on top of existing infrastructure or technology layers, like GPS enabling ride-sharing apps, lowering the bar for the next wave of ideas.

  5. Serendipity and error

    Unplanned mistakes and accidental discoveries are a recurring, productive source of innovation, so environments that tolerate error outperform those optimized purely for efficiency.

  6. The multiple phenomenon

    The same invention is often discovered independently by different people around the same time, because they're all drawing on the same adjacent possible rather than pure individual genius.

Try it this week

  1. Keep a running hunch list where you jot down half-formed ideas, and revisit it monthly instead of discarding ideas that aren't immediately actionable.
  2. Deliberately have one conversation this week with someone outside your usual team or industry to see what adjacent ideas surface.
  3. Audit a recent project for a slow hunch you shelved too early, and ask whether it's now reachable given new tools or information.
  4. Identify one tool or platform your team already has access to and brainstorm one adjacent use nobody has tried yet.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Innovation leaders and product teams designing collaborative environments
  • Readers interested in the history of science and technology

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting a step-by-step personal productivity workbook, since the book is descriptive and historical rather than prescriptive
  • Those focused solely on individual-level creativity techniques rather than organizational or environmental design
