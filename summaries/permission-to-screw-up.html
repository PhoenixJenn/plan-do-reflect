Permission to Screw Up

How I Learned to Lead by Doing (Almost) Everything Wrong

by Kristen Hadeed

2017 · 5h 31m audiobook · ★ 4.8 Audible

The big idea

Drawing on her own near-failure founding the cleaning company Student Maid, Hadeed argues that effective leadership comes not from appearing perfect but from openly owning mistakes and being vulnerable with your team, treating failure as the primary way people, including the leader, learn and grow.

Own-it-fast mistake protocol
When Hadeed or a Student Maid team member makes a mistake, the practice is to admit it openly and quickly, within about 24 hours, explain what happened without excuses, and immediately propose a concrete next step to fix or learn from it. This turns a mistake into a visible trust-building moment for the whole team instead of something hidden or spun, modeling that leadership doesn't require hiding flaws.

Key ideas

  1. Vulnerability is where trust is born

    Leaders sharing their own failures and uncertainties, not just successes, is what makes teams open up in return.

  2. Perfectionism is a leadership liability

    The pressure to look like you have it all figured out prevents the honest conversations that actually solve problems.

  3. Personal relationships come before tasks

    Brief, regular personal check-ins with team members build the trust that holds a team together under pressure.

  4. Invest in people beyond the job description

    Student Maid required employees to attend workshops on relationship-building and conflict skills unrelated to cleaning, building loyalty and a stronger talent pipeline.

  5. Trust your gut in hiring

    Hadeed recounts specific hiring regrets that taught her genuine character and potential can outweigh a checklist of requirements.

  6. Reframe failure as data

    Mistakes and near-failures, including nearly losing the company, are treated as the clearest source of what to change next rather than as reputational threats to manage.

Try it this week

  1. Next time you make a work mistake, tell the affected person within 24 hours, explain what happened plainly, and name your next step, no excuses attached.
  2. Start one 1:1 this week with two minutes of personal check-in before any task talk.
  3. Share one recent professional failure with your team openly, including what you learned, instead of only sharing wins.
  4. Before your next hiring decision, write down what your gut says about the candidate's character separately from their resume checklist, and weigh both.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • First-time or founder-leaders worried about looking incompetent in front of their team
  • Managers of young or early-career teams who want to build a culture of psychological safety

Less useful if

  • Readers wanting rigorous data- or research-driven frameworks, this is a personal narrative-style business book, not an academic one
  • Leaders in highly regulated or high-stakes-error environments where own it fast and move on needs more structure than the book provides
