Effective Communication Skills

by Dalton Kehoe, The Great Courses

2013 · 11h 53m audiobook · ★ 4.4 Audible

The big idea

Most communication breakdowns come from unconscious psychological, biological, and cultural patterns, early conditioning, defensive reactions, ego states, rather than a simple lack of vocabulary, and shifting from control talk to mindful dialogue is what actually fixes them.

Connect, control, dialogue talk
Kehoe frames most conversations as falling into three modes: connect talk, small talk and rapport-building; control talk, commands, blame, and escalation; and dialogue talk, mindful, mutual, collaborative exchange. Recognizing which mode you're in is the first step to shifting toward dialogue.

Runs through all 24 lectures and is paired with other frameworks he cites, including transactional analysis and the ladder of inference.

Key ideas

  1. Talk shapes self

    Self-concept and communication habits are formed young, through childhood conversations with caregivers, and often operate unconsciously in adulthood.

  2. Schema-based perception

    We don't hear what's said so much as we interpret it through mental templates built from past experience, a major source of miscommunication.

  3. Control talk escalates

    Light and heavy control patterns, commands, accusations, blame, are common default reactions under stress and tend to trigger defensiveness.

  4. Active listening is underused

    Genuinely listening, not just waiting to respond, is one of the most neglected and most powerful communication skills.

  5. Gender and cultural patterns

    Discusses report talk versus rapport talk gender differences and broader cultural influences on how people communicate.

  6. Dialogue as practice

    Mindful, compassionate confrontation and structured dialogue are taught as learnable techniques for turning conflict-prone conversations into collaborative ones.

Try it this week

  1. For one day, notice every time you slip into control talk and try re-phrasing it as a question instead.
  2. Practice one I-message this week instead of a you accusation in a tense conversation.
  3. Before responding in your next disagreement, pause and ask yourself which assumption you're filtering the other person's words through.
  4. Try one deliberate active-listening exercise: reflect back what you heard before adding your own point.
