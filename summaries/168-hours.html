168 Hours

by Laura Vanderkam

The big idea

Most people aren't actually short on time. With 168 hours in a week, there's enough room for meaningful work, relationships, health, and leisure if you manage your schedule intentionally instead of filling it with low-value defaults.

The 168-hour time log
For one full week, record everything you do in half-hour increments, being specific (made stir-fry, not dinner). At week's end, tally hours by category and compare what you find to what you assumed you were doing.

Vanderkam's signature exercise. Most people are shocked at the gap between perceived busyness and actual time use, and it's the diagnostic step before any schedule change.

Key ideas

  1. You're not too busy, you're not in control

    Feeling slammed is usually a control problem, not a math problem. The fix is asking whether something is actually a priority before accepting it, not finding more hours.

  2. Build around core competencies

    Identify two or three things you're genuinely best at, at work and at home, and structure your schedule around them, delegating or minimizing the rest.

  3. Outsource the mismatch

    Tasks that don't use your strengths are worth paying to offload if it frees time for higher-value work or family time.

  4. Quality over quantity with family

    Direct, focused time with kids is often only a few hours a day even for engaged parents. The goal is making that time count, not maximizing raw hours together.

  5. Leisure is squandered by default

    Most people have more leisure hours than they think, but much of it disappears into passive, unplanned screen time rather than activities they actually find fulfilling.

Try it this week

  1. Track every activity for the next 168 hours in a simple spreadsheet, in specific detail.
  2. At week's end, total your hours by category and note the biggest surprise.
  3. Write your top three core competencies and one task you could delegate or drop this week to protect time for them.
  4. Pick one recurring low-value time sink and pre-schedule a replacement activity you actually want more of.

Is it for you?

Best for

  • Busy professionals and parents feeling chronically time-strapped
  • People who want a data-driven, not just motivational, approach to time management

Less useful if

  • Readers with tight budgets or rigid schedules; the advice assumes some flexibility to outsource or restructure work
  • Anyone looking for the emotional or psychological side of time pressure rather than the logistics
