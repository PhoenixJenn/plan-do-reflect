Managers & Leaders

"Manager" is a job title. Leadership is a behavior. The two overlap constantly, but they aren't the same thing — and "manager" itself splits into roles that have almost nothing in common with each other.

Try It
1

Manager vs. Leader

Management is a formal role: authority over resources, people, process, or budget, granted by the org chart. Leadership is a behavior: setting direction and moving people toward it, with or without that authority. You can hold the title and never really lead. You can lead with no title at all — anyone on a team who moves it forward is leading, whether or not "manager" is anywhere in their job description.

Management

A role, granted by the organization. Comes with formal authority over some mix of people, budget, or process — and the accountability that comes with it.

Leadership

A behavior, earned through influence. Setting direction and moving people toward it — independent of whether you have formal authority over any of them.

Mark Sanborn makes exactly this case in You Don't Need a Title to Be a Leader: the title is an org-chart fact, the leading is a daily choice, and the second one doesn't wait on the first.

2

Managers of Work vs. People Managers

"Manager" job titles split into two groups that get conflated constantly. One group owns a piece of work — scope, timeline, dependencies — and typically has no direct reports at all. The other owns people — hiring, growth, performance, comp — and is accountable for the humans regardless of what they're currently working on.

Managers of Work — usually no direct reports

Project Manager

Owns a single initiative end to end — scope, timeline, budget, risk. Drives the plan, not the people executing it.

Program Manager

Owns a group of related projects — the dependencies and risk that live between them, not inside any one of them.

Technical Program Manager

A Program Manager with deep technical fluency, usually inside engineering — close enough to the system to drive execution across teams that don't report to them.

People Managers — formal reports, accountable for the humans

Engineering Manager

Formal reports: hiring, growth, performance, comp. Usually also carries technical judgment and unblocks the work — a people manager first, a work coordinator second.

Director / VP

A people manager of people managers — the scope widens from individuals to the org, but the accountability for the humans underneath doesn't change shape, just size.

3

The Outlier: Product Manager

Has "Manager" in the title, and unlike the roles above, the job genuinely spans both columns depending on level — it doesn't sit still the way "Project Manager" or "Engineering Manager" does. What's constant at every level: a PM's job is to prioritize what gets built and drive alignment to strategy, not to own the mechanics of delivery the way a Project or Program Manager does, and not to default into people-management just because the title says "Manager."

IC Product Manager

Owns a single domain or feature. No direct reports, and no formal authority over the engineers or designers building it — moves the team by making the case, not giving an instruction.

Director of Product & Above

Can own an entire product end to end — a website, an app, even something the scale of Prime Video — with other Product Managers reporting to them, each owning a narrower domain underneath. A real people manager at this point, on top of everything below.

The influence-only IC version and the people-manager Director version look like different jobs day to day — but the throughline holds at both: own the prioritization and the strategic "why," whether or not you also own a team.
Watch-out

Knowing which kind of manager you are — or want to become — changes what you should actually practice. Growing as a Project or Program Manager means getting sharper at scope, dependencies, and risk. Growing as an Engineering Manager means getting better at hiring, coaching, and hard conversations. They're different jobs wearing the same job-title word, and conflating them is how a strong IC gets promoted into a people-manager role they never wanted.

Try It

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

Help me get clear on whether I'm built for managing work or managing people, or whether I'm actually leading without either kind of formal authority (like a Product Manager does). Ask me about my current role, what parts of it energize me versus drain me, and whether I actually want direct reports — then tell me which track (work-manager, people-manager, or influence-only) fits best and what I should develop next for it.
