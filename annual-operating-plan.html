Annual Operating Plan

The planning cycle at company scale — budget, headcount, and priorities set for the year ahead.

Organization Plan Try It

Where Strategic Planning sets the direction and OKRs make it measurable, the Annual Operating Plan is where it actually gets paid for — real dollars assigned to real teams, for a real year. Four pieces make it up:

  • Budget — operating and capital spend allocated by team or initiative for the year. Set through a mix of top-down targets from Finance and bottom-up asks from each team, reconciled before the plan locks — see Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up below.
  • Headcount Plan — the people behind the budget: new hires, backfills, and where roles move. Usually the most contested line in the whole plan — every initiative wants net-new headcount, and there's rarely enough to fund all of them.
  • Priorities Cascade — the ranked list of initiatives that actually get funded this year, pulled straight from Strategic Planning's Winning Aspiration and OKRs. Not everything that's strategically "nice" makes the cut. This is where ambition meets a finite budget.
  • Calendar & Milestones — the planning season itself, plus the checkpoints through the year where the plan gets checked against reality. Most companies run planning in Q3/Q4 for the following fiscal year, then revisit at the mid-year mark if the landscape has shifted — see Adjust on Strategic Planning.

Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up

The two ways a budget actually gets built. Most real plans are some blend of both:

  • Top-Down — leadership sets a target first (flat headcount, 10% opex growth) and teams plan within it. Faster, but risks setting a number before anyone's checked whether it's actually enough. Zero-based budgeting — building every line from zero instead of last year's number — is a stricter version of this, used aggressively by 3G Capital at Kraft Heinz and Anheuser-Busch InBev.
  • Bottom-Up — each team builds its own ask from what it actually needs, then those get rolled up and reconciled against what the company can afford. More accurate, slower, and almost always needs a top-down number to converge against — otherwise the asks just add up to more than the company can spend.
Example

Team: Growth Marketing

Ask: $2.4M budget, 3 net-new FTEs

Approved: $1.8M budget, 1 net-new FTE + 1 backfill

Try It

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

Help me sanity-check a budget ask for next year. Ask me what I'm asking for — budget and headcount — and what it's for. Then push me to defend it bottom-up: what specifically breaks if I don't get it? Finally, help me rank my asks in case I only get partial funding, so I know what to protect first.
