This isn't the deep research discipline — interviews, Jobs to Be Done, opportunity-solution trees belong to Research, a separate skill. This is the fast, practical version: what to do in week one, before you've earned the time for a full research program, so you're not writing a Vision Statement's Target Customer field from a guess.

  • Audit What Already Exists — read support tickets, sales call notes, churn data, NPS/CSAT, usage analytics, and social media or review mentions before running anything new. For consumer products, a customer satisfaction or social-listening team is often already mining this and has a dashboard built — find it before starting from zero.
  • Talk to Internal Stakeholders First — sales, support, and customer success already have pattern-matched customer knowledge. Borrow it before generating new research from scratch.
  • Segment the Customer Base — not every user of the product is the same customer. Figure out which segment actually matters right now, not just which one's loudest.
  • Get Direct Customer Contact — nothing replaces hearing it yourself. A secondhand summary always loses the detail that mattered.
  • Synthesize into a Customer Profile — turn scattered inputs into one usable page. This becomes the actual Target Customer field on Product Vision & Strategy.

The most common mistake isn't skipping research — it's skipping the free research that's already sitting in a ticket queue. Talking to five customers before reading two hundred existing support tickets means re-discovering what your own team already knew.

Example: iPodExample: RouteWise, week one

Audit What Already Exists: Apple didn't have iPod tickets yet — but the Mac team already had years of data on how loyalists ripped CDs and organized sprawling MP3 folders by hand.

Audit What Already Exists: Two hundred RouteWise support tickets tagged "POD" had been sitting unread — every one was a driver losing a proof-of-delivery photo.

Talk to Internal Stakeholders First: The team already building a digital jukebox app for the Mac knew exactly where existing MP3 players failed — slow transfers and tiny storage, not the music itself.

Talk to Internal Stakeholders First: Sales already knew RouteWise's #1 lost-deal reason — no offline mode for drivers working in warehouses with no signal.

Segment the Customer Base: Not every music fan was the target — the focus was people who already owned a large, organized digital library, not someone buying their first single-artist player.

Segment the Customer Base: Two very different users share one RouteWise login — dispatchers who plan routes at a desk, and drivers who need three buttons that work with gloves on.

Get Direct Customer Contact: Early prototypes were tested against how people actually loaded and organized their own libraries, not how an engineer assumed they would.

Get Direct Customer Contact: Five driver ride-alongs surfaced the offline problem before a single dashboard chart did.

Synthesize into a Customer Profile: The profile came down to one line — someone who already owns the music and just wants all of it, everywhere, without the hassle.

Synthesize into a Customer Profile: One page — "drivers, mid-shift, spotty signal, gloves on" — became the actual Target Customer field on the vision doc.

"A customer complained" isn't a scored opportunity — it's a complaint with a vote count. Six steps to turn a pile of pain points into the short list that actually earns a Vision Statement's Problem/Opportunity field:

  • List Every Candidate Problem — pull every complaint, ticket theme, and stakeholder pain point onto one unranked list before judging any of them. Also include friction nobody's complaining about — customers stop mentioning what they've quietly gotten used to — and emerging technology that just became newly possible, even with no customer signal at all.
  • Score Frequency & Severity — how often does this happen, and how bad is it when it does? A rare-but-catastrophic problem and a frequent-but-minor one don't compete without both numbers.
  • Estimate Reach — how many customers, or how much of the business, does this actually touch. A painful problem hitting 2% of customers loses to a mild one hitting 60%, more often than people expect.
  • Separate Root Cause from Symptom — is this the actual problem, or a downstream symptom of something already on the list? Fixing a symptom twice wastes the effort of fixing it once.
  • Check Strategic Fit — does solving this ladder up to where Product Vision & Strategy is already trying to go, or is it a distraction dressed as an opportunity?
  • Cut to a Short List — narrow the candidates down to the few worth carrying into Product Vision & Strategy's Problem/Opportunity field.

Three things to watch for while building the candidate list — each one easy to miss in a different way:

The Quiet One

What customers complain about loudest isn't always the real problem. The classic case is the slow-elevator complaint — building managers who tried fixing it by making elevators literally faster spent a fortune for marginal gains. The complaint was about speed; the actual problem was boredom during the wait. Installing mirrors next to the elevators (later, TVs) didn't shave a second off the ride, but complaints dropped anyway, because people stopped noticing the wait once they had something to look at.

The Loud One

The biggest opportunity is often something nobody complains about at all, because they've quietly adapted to the friction and stopped noticing it — you can't complain about the absence of something that isn't in your vocabulary yet. Amazon's 1-Click checkout, Uber's live map showing your driver approaching, Netflix's "Skip Intro" button, and 3M's Post-it Note (a failed strong-adhesive experiment employees started using internally long before anyone asked for temporary sticky notes) all removed friction or created value nobody had a name for yet. A candidate list built only from complaints will always miss opportunities like that.

Good Timing

A third source doesn't start from a customer signal at all — it starts from noticing that two unrelated pieces of technology have just become available at the same time. The iPod is the real example: Apple's head of hardware noticed in early 2001 that Toshiba had just built a tiny 1.8-inch hard drive with no product to put it in, and an unclaimed chipset reference design already existed — the pieces to build a real portable music player suddenly existed, and nobody else had noticed they fit together. This kind of opportunity won't show up in a complaint list or a ticket queue, because the product it enables doesn't exist yet for anyone to complain about. It comes from watching what's newly possible, not just what's newly painful — exactly what a Research practice should be scanning for, alongside customer interviews.

The most common failure is skipping straight from "a customer complained" to "we should build X" — that's a solution wearing a problem's clothing. Score the problem before anyone's allowed to pitch a fix for it.

Example: iPodExample: RouteWise

List Every Candidate Problem: Candidates on the table — too few songs fit, transfers were painfully slow, batteries died fast, and interfaces were clumsy. Four different problems, all mixed together.

List Every Candidate Problem: Candidates on the table — lost proof-of-delivery photos, drivers going offline in warehouses, slow onboarding, and confusing route-reassignment screens.

Score Frequency & Severity: Slow transfers hit every single use, every time; short battery life was annoying but only on longer days away from a charger — frequency won.

Score Frequency & Severity: Lost proof-of-delivery photos happened on nearly every shift with a warehouse stop; confusing reassignment screens were annoying but rare — both numbers pointed the same direction.

Estimate Reach: Low storage capacity affected anyone with more than a handful of CDs already ripped — nearly every existing MP3 player owner, not a niche.

Estimate Reach: The offline problem touched every driver on every route with a low-signal stop — closer to 80% of shifts than anyone on the team had assumed.

Separate Root Cause from Symptom: Clumsy interfaces and slow transfers both traced back to the same root cause — nobody had designed the hardware and software together as one system.

Separate Root Cause from Symptom: Lost photos and stalled route status were two symptoms of the same root cause — the app assumed a live connection existed for the whole shift.

Check Strategic Fit: A device that solved storage and speed fit a hardware-plus-software company's actual advantage; a fix that required every other manufacturer to adopt a shared standard didn't.

Check Strategic Fit: Fixing offline reliability matched RouteWise's actual differentiation bet; redesigning the reassignment screen was a real annoyance but didn't ladder up to anything strategic yet.

Cut to a Short List: One sentence — capacity and speed, solved together, for people who already owned real libraries. Everything else could wait for a later release.

Cut to a Short List: One problem — proof-of-delivery and route status have to survive zero signal for a full shift. Everything else got parked for a later cycle.

A vision without a strategy is a poster. A strategy without a vision is a to-do list. Fill in these eight fields to connect the two, and revisit them whenever a roadmap decision needs a tiebreaker:

  • Vision Statement — one sentence, the future state you're aiming at (3-5 years out), not a list of things to ship.
  • Target Customer — who this is for, and just as important, who it's explicitly not for.
  • Problem / Opportunity — the unmet need in the market that justifies the vision.
  • Why Now — what's already true that makes this winnable today, not five years ago or five years from now. Two different flavors, and a strong case often has both: technology (a new component or capability just became available) and culture (the market has already normalized the behavior this needs).
  • Strategic Bets — the 2-4 big choices this cycle that ladder up to the vision. A bet is something you could be wrong about — if it's not risky, it's not a bet, it's a feature.
  • Differentiation — why this, and not a competitor or the status quo.
  • Non-Goals — deliberately out of scope. Naming what you won't do protects the bets you did make.
  • Success Measure — how you'll know the strategy is working.

The most common failure isn't a bad vision statement — it's strategic bets that are actually just a feature list wearing a strategy costume. A real bet names a belief that could turn out to be wrong.

Example: iPodExample: RouteWise

Vision: Every song you own, in your pocket, always with you.

Vision: Every delivery, tracked and proven, even when the driver's phone has zero bars.

Target Customer: People who already own a real music collection and are tired of lugging around a CD binder — not casual listeners happy with the radio.

Target Customer: Regional last-mile delivery companies running 50-500 drivers — not solo couriers, and not massive carriers who'll build this in-house.

Problem / Opportunity: Existing MP3 players in 2001 held a dozen songs and took forever to load. Nobody had solved "my whole library, always with me."

Problem / Opportunity: Dispatchers were flying blind the moment a driver walked into a warehouse with no signal — routes silently stalled and nobody knew until a customer called angry.

Why Now (technology): Toshiba had just built a tiny 1.8-inch, 5GB hard drive with no product to put it in, and an unclaimed chipset reference design already existed — the pieces to build this suddenly existed, waiting for someone to notice they fit together.

Why Now (technology): Cheap Android hardware and larger on-device storage finally made a real offline mode possible — five years earlier, the phones dispatchers issued couldn't have held a full day's route data locally.

Why Now (culture): The Sony Walkman had already normalized wearing headphones and listening to music in public — the iPod didn't have to create that behavior, just make it better.

Why Now (culture): Regional carriers had already shifted to a gig-style, high-turnover driver workforce — a dead-simple app that just needed to work reliably fit how drivers already operated, no retraining required.

Strategic Bet: Bet on a tightly closed hardware-plus-iTunes system over an open, mix-and-match MP3 player market — control the whole experience end to end.

Strategic Bet: Bet on offline-first over always-connected — build as if signal is the exception, not the assumption, even though it makes the engineering harder.

Differentiation: A 5GB hard drive, a scroll wheel you could operate one-handed, and FireWire sync fast enough to load 1,000 songs in under 10 minutes.

Differentiation: The only driver app in the category that keeps working — full route data, proof-of-delivery capture, status updates — with zero signal for an entire shift.

Non-Goals: Not a phone, not a video player, not a photo album — just music, done better than anyone else, at launch.

Non-Goals: Not a fleet-maintenance tool, not a payroll system, not a customer-facing tracking page — just get the delivery proven, reliably, every time.

Success Measure: iPod becomes the default way people carry their music, not just another player on the shelf.

Success Measure: Proof-of-delivery capture succeeds on the first try 99% of the time, signal or no signal.

A Strategic Bet from Product Vision & Strategy is still a sentence, not a spec. A PRD is where it becomes buildable — seven fields to fill in before engineering starts estimating:

  • Problem Statement — the specific problem this solves, one level more concrete than the strategy-level Problem/Opportunity it came from.
  • Goals & Success Criteria — what shipped-and-working actually looks like, measurably.
  • User Stories — as a [user], I want [action], so that [outcome]. The functional requirements, written from the user's side.
  • Acceptance Criteria — the specific, testable conditions that make each story countable as done.
  • Scope (In / Out) — what's included in this release, and what's explicitly deferred.
  • Assumptions & Dependencies — what has to be true, or which other teams and systems this relies on, for the plan to hold.
  • Open Questions — what's still unresolved, tracked instead of silently guessed at.

The most common PRD failure is acceptance criteria vague enough that everyone can claim they were met. "The app should load quickly" isn't testable. "The app loads in under 2 seconds on a 4G connection" is.

Example: iPodExample: RouteWise

Problem Statement: Users can't carry more than a handful of songs without lugging a separate device for every format.

Problem Statement: Drivers lose proof-of-delivery photos and dispatchers can't see route status the moment a driver loses signal.

Goals & Success Criteria: Ship a device that holds 1,000 songs and syncs a full library in under 10 minutes.

Goals & Success Criteria: Ship offline mode that captures and locally queues proof-of-delivery for an entire shift with zero signal, syncing automatically the moment signal returns.

User Stories: As a commuter, I want to load my whole CD collection onto one device, so that I never have to choose which album to bring.

User Stories: As a driver, I want to keep completing my route with no signal, so that I'm not stuck waiting or making a customer wait while my phone reconnects.

Acceptance Criteria: A 4-minute song transfers in under 2 seconds over FireWire; the device holds at least 1,000 typical MP3s at 128kbps.

Acceptance Criteria: A full 8-hour shift of stops, photos, and signatures queues locally and syncs within 30 seconds of regaining signal, with zero data loss.

Scope (In / Out): In: music playback and sync. Out: photos, video, and any wireless features — those wait for a future release.

Scope (In / Out): In: offline proof-of-delivery capture and local queueing. Out: offline route re-optimization — that still requires a live connection, for this release.

Assumptions & Dependencies: Depends on iTunes shipping in time for launch, and on FireWire ports being standard on the target Mac lineup.

Assumptions & Dependencies: Depends on drivers' issued devices having at least 2GB of free local storage, and on the backend sync API supporting batched, out-of-order updates.

Open Questions: Still unresolved at spec time — does the scroll wheel need to be touch-sensitive, or will a physical click suffice?

Open Questions: Still unresolved at spec time — what happens if a driver's queued data never syncs before their shift resets at midnight?

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I'm writing a Product Requirements Document for a feature or product. Ask me what I'm building first, then walk me through drafting each field one at a time: Problem Statement (the specific problem this solves, in one or two sentences), Goals & Success Criteria (what shipped-and-working looks like, measurably), User Stories (as a [user], I want [action], so that [outcome]), Acceptance Criteria (specific, testable conditions for each story), Scope (what's explicitly in this release vs. deferred), Assumptions & Dependencies (what has to be true, or which other teams and systems this relies on), and Open Questions (what's still unresolved). Push back if any acceptance criterion isn't actually testable — "should feel fast" doesn't count, a number does.
