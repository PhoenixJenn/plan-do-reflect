Goal Setting & Priorities

Time management tells you how to spend your hours. This is what decides which hours are worth spending at all.

The Framework 6 frameworks

Why It Matters

Skip this step and even a perfectly organized calendar just executes the wrong things efficiently. This is the Plan half of the cycle — decide what you're actually aiming for, and which few things matter most, before you start protecting time for any of it. Do it well and Time Management has something worth defending; skip it and the best productivity system in the world just helps you do more of whatever showed up first.

Frameworks We Cover

Six ways to decide what actually matters before you commit time to it — the first two set the destination, the rest decide what earns your attention on the way there. A few other prioritization frameworks are worth knowing but similar enough to what's here that they don't get their own write-up yet: Pickle Jar Theory, the MoSCoW Method, RICE Scoring, the Kano Model, and the 1-3-5 Method.

1. Set Goals — longer-term, where you're actually headed
1

SMART Goals

Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Less a framework than a filter — run a goal through it before committing time to it, and vague intentions ("get better at this") turn into something you can actually plan around and know when you've hit.

2

OKRs (Objectives & Key Results)

Pick 1–3 objectives — what you actually want to accomplish — and 3–5 measurable key results per objective that tell you whether you got there. The same framework companies run at the org level, scaled down to a personal goal: an objective without a measurable key result is just a wish.

💡

"What's the one thing I can do such that by doing it everything else will become easier or unnecessary?"

— Gary Keller & Jay Papasan, The ONE Thing

The ONE Thing calls this the Domino Effect: success stacks like a line of falling dominoes — hit the first, most important target, and it knocks down the smaller tasks that depended on it. It's as much a mindset shift as a technique: measuring yourself by what you actually produced, not by how busy you looked getting there.

2. Prioritize Work — shorter-term, day-to-day
3

The 80/20 Rule (Pareto Principle)

Roughly 20% of your effort drives 80% of your results — named after economist Vilfredo Pareto, and it shows up everywhere: resources, time, customers, projects. For individuals, it means auditing your week and doubling down on the few activities that actually move things forward. For teams and companies, it's the same logic applied to which customers, products, or initiatives get the attention. The habit underneath it: simplify your goals to fewer priorities, pursue them with clarity and consistency, and cut the energy drains — extra meetings, low-value tasks, draining people — that don't earn their place in the 20%.

4

Eisenhower Matrix

Sort everything into four boxes: urgent + important (do it now), important but not urgent (schedule it), urgent but not important (delegate it), neither (delete it). The line worth remembering: what's important is rarely urgent, and what's urgent is rarely important — which is exactly why this matrix exists instead of just working off whatever's loudest.

5

Most Important Things (MITs)

Pick your 3 most important tasks for the week and get them done — everything else is negotiable, these aren't. A simple constraint that keeps a long list from quietly replacing an actual priority.

6

Weekly Planning Ritual

30 minutes on a Sunday night can save 10 hours in the week ahead. Sit down before Monday starts and decide what matters, so the week doesn't decide it for you — the same idea shows up in how senior leaders describe planning their own weeks, just applied at whatever scale your work runs at.
