Strategic Planning

The planning cycle zoomed out further still: scan the landscape, formulate, cascade into plans, measure, adjust.

Organization 5 steps Try It

Not a single framework but a cycle, the same way the rest of this site is. Each step below answers a different question, and skipping one usually shows up as a failure in a later one — a company that never scanned the landscape discovers its "How to Win" doesn't hold up, and one that never cascaded its vision into real choices ends up with a Mission statement nobody can act on.

1

Scan the Landscape

Two passes: name what you're actually optimizing for, then read the competitive pressure on it.

Set the Strategic Theme

Almost every strategic plan is ultimately in service of one of four value drivers. Naming the one that's primary first keeps the scan, the choices, and the metrics that follow all pointed at the same target instead of pulling four directions at once:

  • Grow Revenue — new markets, new products, pricing. Amazon folding Prime into groceries, ads, and healthcare is this at trillion-dollar scale; Microsoft's pivot from on-prem licensing to Azure and, more recently, Copilot/AI is the same driver pursued through outright reinvention.
  • Reduce Cost — efficiency, automation, supply chain. Walmart's decades-long investment in logistics and automation; GE's push to put sensors and predictive software on jet engines and turbines, cutting maintenance costs through data instead of brute-force efficiency.
  • Reduce Risk — security, compliance, resilience. JPMorgan's multi-billion-dollar annual cybersecurity spend; Boeing's post-737 MAX overhaul of its engineering and safety culture — transformation forced by existential risk rather than chosen ahead of it.
  • Improve Customer Experience — retention, loyalty, satisfaction. Costco's near-obsessive focus on its membership renewal rate; Starbucks rebuilding itself around Mobile Order & Pay and its rewards app, turning a coffee line into a data-driven loyalty engine.
  • Other (AI, emerging tech, etc.) — pressure that doesn't map cleanly to the other four yet. Most companies' current AI strategy is driven by "we need a position" more than a proven ROI case — which value driver it actually serves gets sorted out after the capability exists, not before.

Read the Pressure — Porter's Five Forces

A framework for reading the competitive pressure on the theme you just picked, not just naming direct competitors:

  • Threat of new entrants — how easily fresh competitors can show up and undercut on price.
  • Supplier power — how much suppliers can squeeze you on price or quality if you're dependent on them.
  • Buyer power — how much customers can demand lower prices or better terms.
  • Threat of substitutes — alternative ways to solve the same problem that aren't direct competitors.
  • Rivalry among existing competitors — the force at the center, shaped by the other four.
Watch-out

The force actually hurting you isn't always the obvious one. A café losing customers isn't necessarily losing them to other cafés — it might be losing them to delivery apps (a substitute), and the fix is competing on experience and loyalty, not price.

2

Formulate — Mission, Vision & Values

Three related but distinct statements, usually confused for one another:

  • Mission — why you exist right now: the problem you solve, for whom, today.
  • Vision — the future state you're working toward if the mission succeeds.
  • Values — how you operate and make decisions while getting there, especially when holding them is costly.

This is one rung above where most individual teams start — Team's Product Vision & Strategy takes company Mission/Vision/Values as a given and translates them into one product's bets.

Examples
Apple
  • Mission: make tools that amplify human creativity and capability.
  • Vision: technology so intuitive it disappears into the experience.
  • Values: design and privacy held as non-negotiable, even when they slow a launch down.
Patagonia
  • Mission: in business to save our home planet.
  • Vision: a future where business gives back more to nature than it takes.
  • Values: environmental activism over quarterly growth — including its own ads telling customers not to buy a jacket they didn't need.
LEGO
  • Mission: inspire and develop the builders of tomorrow.
  • Vision: invent the future of play.
  • Values: "only the best is good enough" — quality and imagination over shipping fast.
3

Cascade Into Plans — The Strategic Choice Cascade

From Playing to Win (Lafley & Martin): five sequential, interdependent choices. Each answer constrains the next — a Where-to-Play choice made without a clear How-to-Win is just a target list, not a strategy.

  1. Winning Aspiration — what does winning actually look like?
  2. Where to Play — which markets, segments, and customers.
  3. How to Win — the specific competitive advantage in that arena.
  4. Core Capabilities — what must be true internally to win that way.
  5. Management Systems — the metrics, incentives, and governance that build and sustain those capabilities.
4

Measure

The cascade's choices become trackable the moment they're written as OKRs — Winning Aspiration becomes an Objective, and the capabilities and systems underneath it become Key Results everyone can see progress against.

5

Adjust

The regular, scheduled check against reality — MBR & QBR — is what actually closes the loop. A plan that's never revisited against what happened isn't a strategy, it's a prediction nobody checked.

Try It

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

Help me run a lightweight strategic planning pass for my team. First ask me which strategic theme is primary right now — Grow Revenue, Reduce Cost, Reduce Risk, Improve Customer Experience, or Other (AI, emerging tech, etc.) — and have me defend the choice. Then ask me questions to scan the landscape through that lens using Porter's Five Forces. Then help me state our Mission, Vision, and Values in one sentence each. Finally, walk me through the Strategic Choice Cascade — Winning Aspiration, Where to Play, How to Win, Core Capabilities, Management Systems — pushing me to make real, exclusionary choices rather than restating my aspirations.
