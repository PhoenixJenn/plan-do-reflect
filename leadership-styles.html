Leadership Styles

There's no one right way to lead — only the right way for this person, this moment. The best leaders carry a repertoire, not a personality.

Try It

Researchers and practitioners have named leadership styles under a lot of different labels over the decades, and most of them describe overlapping territory rather than competing theories. Four groupings cover the useful ground: Goleman's six situational styles (the most actionable day-to-day toolkit), Situational Leadership's follower-readiness model, the transformational/transactional split, and a handful of other classic named styles worth recognizing when you hear them.

"Leadership is influence, nothing more, nothing less."

— John C. Maxwell, The 21 Irrefutable Laws of Leadership
1

Goleman's Six Leadership Styles

From Daniel Goleman's research on emotional intelligence and leadership: four styles build long-term climate and trust ("resonant"), and two get fast results but burn people out if they're your default rather than your exception ("dissonant" — use sparingly, on purpose).

Visionary (Authoritative)

"Here's where we're going — figure out how." Mobilizes people toward a shared vision and gives them room to run.

Best when: direction is unclear and the team needs a destination, not a to-do list.

Watch for: it can read as a mandate from above if you announce the vision instead of selling it.

Coaching

"Try this." Invests in someone's long-term growth, connecting what they want with what the team needs.

Best when: someone's motivated and open to building a specific skill.

Watch for: it's slow — the wrong move when a decision is needed right now.

Affiliative

"People come first." Builds emotional bonds, heals rifts, and boosts morale.

Best when: trust is low or the team's been through a rough stretch.

Watch for: it avoids hard feedback and lets mediocre performance slide.

Democratic

"What do you think?" Builds buy-in through participation and surfaces ideas you wouldn't have had alone.

Best when: you genuinely need the team's input or buy-in to succeed.

Watch for: endless meetings, and no decision when speed actually matters.

Pacesetting

"Do as I do, right now." Sets a high bar and models it personally, expecting the team to keep up.

Best when: a skilled, self-motivated team needs a fast result.

Watch for: Goleman's own research flags this as the most overused, under-delivering style — a sprint, not a default.

Commanding (Coercive)

"Do what I tell you." Demands immediate compliance, no discussion.

Best when: a genuine crisis, or a problem situation nothing else has reached.

Watch for: the most damaging style on team climate if it's your everyday mode instead of your emergency brake.

Situational Leadership

Hersey and Blanchard's model: the right style isn't about you at all — it's about how ready the person is for the specific task in front of them. The same person might need Directing on a task they're new to and Delegating on one they've mastered, in the same week.

3

Transformational vs. Transactional

Burns and Bass's split, operating one level up from the styles above — less "how do I run this conversation" and more "what's the overall deal between me and the team."

Transactional

Leadership as an exchange — clear expectations, rewards for hitting them, correction for missing them. Keeps the day-to-day running predictably.

Transformational

Leadership that connects the work to something bigger than the task — inspiring people to stretch past what they thought they could do. What actually drives change.

Most effective leaders run both: transactional keeps the trains running, transformational is what gets you somewhere genuinely new.

4

Other Named Styles Worth Recognizing

Older and adjacent frameworks you'll still hear by name. Kurt Lewin's original 1939 study named three: his Democratic maps directly onto Goleman's Democratic above, and Autocratic overlaps heavily with Commanding — Laissez-Faire is the one that doesn't have a clean Goleman equivalent.

Autocratic

Decisions made solely by the leader, with little to no input sought. Fast, but only as good as the one person making the call.

Laissez-Faire

Hands-off — the leader provides resources and steps back, trusting the team to self-direct. Works only when the team is genuinely capable; otherwise it reads as absent.

Servant Leadership

The leader's job is to serve the team first — remove obstacles, develop people, put their needs ahead of their own. A philosophy as much as a technique, and the root a lot of modern coaching-style management borrows from.

Charismatic

Leads through personal magnetism and conviction rather than position or process. Powerful for rallying people around a cause — and risky, since the vision lives in one person and doesn't easily outlast them.

Bureaucratic

Leads by the book — policy, procedure, and hierarchy decide, not personal judgment. The right call in regulated or safety-critical environments; the wrong one almost everywhere else.

Strategic (Visionary)

Operates at the organization level rather than the team level — setting long-range direction for the business itself. Overlaps with Goleman's Visionary above, scaled up from a team to a company.

Watch-out

These aren't fixed personalities to pick once and keep. The leaders with the best track records aren't the ones who found "their style" and stuck with it — they're the ones with the broadest range, who read the situation and deliberately switch. Defaulting to whatever feels most natural is usually a sign you haven't built the other ones yet.
