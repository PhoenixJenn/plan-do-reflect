Project & Program Management

Planning a specific initiative before you run it — from a fuzzy idea to a chartered, resourced, trackable piece of work.

A good charter prevents a fuzzy project. Fill in these nine fields before work begins, and revisit it throughout the project for scope checks and trade-off decisions — not just once at kickoff:

Problem Statement — what's actually wrong, in one sentence.

Goal — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.

Business Case — why this is worth doing.

Scope — what's in, and just as important, what's out.

Tenets — short, memorable principles for how the team should decide when the charter doesn't spell it out. "Unless you know better ones" keeps them a guide, not a law.

Team — sponsor, team lead, process owner, stakeholders.

Timeline — the major milestones, not a full schedule. Include a checkpoint for the Launch & Communications Plan, not just launch day itself — the most common miss is waiting too long to engage Marketing, internal Comms, Customer Success, or Legal, and finding out on launch day that one of them needed weeks of lead time.

Metrics — how you'll know it worked.

Risks — what could derail it.

Vague problem statements and weak sponsor alignment are the two most common ways a charter fails — a charter without a clearly accountable sponsor tends to stall the moment the project hits its first blocker.

Sample Goal: Reduce invoice approval cycle time from 10 days to 4 days within 90 days.

Sample Tenets (unless you know better ones):
1. "Default to self-service over a manual approval step."
2. "When speed and polish conflict before launch, speed wins."

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I'm kicking off a new project. Ask me about the problem I'm trying to solve, then walk me through drafting a Project Charter one field at a time — Problem Statement, Goal, Business Case, Scope, Tenets (short guiding principles for decisions the charter doesn't spell out — "unless you know better ones"), Team, Timeline, Metrics, and Risks. Push back if any of my answers are too vague.

A project plan is not project management strategy — the critical difference most project leaders miss. Strategy is the why: the operating logic for how the project creates value. Plan is the how: the execution roadmap that converts strategy into activities, owners, and milestones.

StrategyPlan
What it isHow the project creates value, aligns stakeholders, and adapts under uncertaintyThe roadmap that converts strategy into activities, owners, milestones, and controls
Time horizonProject lifecycle through benefits realization; reset at major stage gatesShort-term execution cycles — updated continuously by stage, sprint, or milestone
FlexibilityStable direction with tailored delivery optionsChanges frequently as execution conditions shift
When to useDefine outcomes, select a delivery approach, align sponsors and governanceRun phases and sprints, coordinate assignments, track scope/budget/risk
Common mistakeTreating the schedule as the strategyOver-detailing tasks too early, tracking activity instead of outcomes

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I need to define the Strategy for a project, not just write a plan for it. Ask me what problem I'm actually trying to solve, then push me to name the why behind it before we touch any how — the real diagnosis, not a goal in disguise. Once that's solid, help me write a guiding policy: the overall approach I'm choosing and why it beats the alternatives. Only then, sketch the first few coherent actions that follow from it, and call out anything that's really just a task list or a vision statement wearing a strategy costume.

Once Strategy has settled the why, this is where the how actually gets built: checkpoints along the way, a sequenced schedule between them, checked against real capacity, with a risk register for what could still break it.

Milestones & Checkpoints Sprint Review / Increment

A schedule tells you when things finish. Milestones tell you whether the project is actually working before it's too late to change course — checkpoints where you can show something real, not just report a status. The best ones deliver measurable value on their own, not just progress toward some future deliverable.

Kickoff

Charter signed, team staffed

First Increment

Something a real user could actually touch

Checkpoint Review

Compare against the plan, adjust if needed

Launch

The outcome actually ships

Rinse & Repeat

Launch doesn't have to be the end of the track — for most real projects it's one loop of several. Ship multiple MLP milestones instead of a single big-bang release: cycle back through First Increment, Checkpoint Review, and Launch again for the next slice of value. That way, if the project gets deprioritized or cut at any point along the way, the team has already shipped something real, not just spent the budget on the way to a finish line it never reached.

Critical Path Method

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks in a project — the sequence that determines the earliest possible finish date. It's not always the path with the biggest individual tasks; it's whichever path adds up to the most days total. Every other path has slack — room to slip a little without moving the finish date. A simple example: launching a new checkout page.

Critical path (Kickoff → Backend API → Integration → Launch) — 11 days, zero slack Parallel path (Kickoff → Design → Frontend → Integration) — 10 days, 1 day of slack

Design (2d) and Frontend build (4d), plus a day to hand Frontend off into Integration, add up to 7 days — still faster than Backend API's 8 days on its own, even though it's two extra nodes to get there. But Integration can't start until both branches are done, so it's stuck waiting on Backend API regardless. Frontend finishing early doesn't buy the project much; it just sits on 1 day of slack. The finish date is set by whichever chain totals the most days end-to-end — not by which one has the fewest hops, and not by which one looks faster.

Those day counts have to come from somewhere. Most teams get them from Story Pointing — sizing each task's relative effort, often on a Fibonacci-ish scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13), then converting points to rough duration once the team's actual velocity is known. Get the points wrong and the whole critical path is fiction, however precise it looks on a chart.

Tracer Bullets

From The Pragmatic Programmer: before fully building out every task on the critical path, build a thin, working version that touches every node from Start to End — even if each piece is bare-bones. It proves the sequence and dependencies actually hold before real effort gets sunk into any one of them. Named for tracer ammunition: you see immediately whether you're aiming right, not just whether the gun fired.

Six ways to compress the critical path without losing quality:

Re-sequence tasks — adjust the order of dependent tasks to eliminate unnecessary waiting.

Parallelize with a contract — split into workstreams that build side by side, like Frontend and Backend API above, but only works if you plan the seam first. Define the interface between them — the API handshake — and mock it out, so each side builds against the contract instead of waiting on the other to finish.

Test in place, not just at the end — with parallel workstreams, QA doesn't have to wait for Integration. Testing each increment as it lands catches problems while they're still cheap to fix, instead of stacking all the quality risk onto one crunch at the merge point.

Add resources selectively — assign another skilled contributor to the longest-pole tasks specifically.

Reduce scope where possible — cut or simplify non-essential features that sit on the critical path.

Remove approval delays — streamline review and approval steps that are blocking movement, not adding value.

Do not compress work blindly — quality and dependency logic still matter.

Reality Check

Capacity & Resource Planning Gotchas — not a step you complete once, a standing discipline you keep re-running against the schedule above. Revisit it weekly, not just at kickoff.

Planning at 100% capacity — leaves no room for interruptions. Fix: plan for 70–80% utilization instead.

Ignoring recurring meetings — overestimates available hours. Fix: account for standing meetings and reporting commitments.

Assuming all specialists are interchangeable — leads to skill gaps. Fix: plan by role and proficiency, not just headcount.

Missing vacation calendars — creates preventable schedule surprises. Fix: integrate time-off and holiday calendars into planning.

Not separating project work from BAU/KTLO — hides how much time is really available. Fix: track project work and business-as-usual work separately.

Overlooking key specialists — single points of failure go unnoticed. Fix: identify and calendar-block your bottleneck skill sets first.

Principles of effective capacity management: transparency, built-in buffers, realistic estimates, and a weekly review cadence.

Risk Management

For each risk that could derail the plan: how likely is it, how bad if it happens, and what are you actually going to do about it?

Avoid — change the plan so the risk can't happen at all.

Mitigate — reduce the likelihood, the impact, or both.

Transfer — shift the risk elsewhere: a vendor contract, insurance, a more experienced team.

Accept — acknowledge it and move on, when managing it would cost more than the risk itself.

Run it as a cycle, not a one-time exercise: identify → assess → respond → monitor. The most common failure mode isn't missing a risk — it's writing it down once and never looking at it again.

For programs and larger initiatives where a single register isn't enough — five lenses to view risk through instead of one flat list:

Strategic Risk — threats to growth, market position, and long-term direction. Best for board and executive decisions.

Operational Resilience — disruption risk across processes, people, systems, and facilities. Best for continuity and service reliability.

Cyber & Data Risk — digital exposure from attacks, data loss, and technology failure. Best for security and control focus.

Third-Party Dependency — risk created by vendors, platforms, and critical partners. Best for supplier and ecosystem oversight.

AI & Model Risk — uncertainty from automated decisions and weak governance. Best for AI adoption and assurance.

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

Plan the logic before you commit to the dates. This isn't a checklist — it's a hierarchy. Start with the outcome that justifies the work, then keep splitting it into smaller, ownable pieces until you hit assignable tasks. If your team runs Agile, you already know this hierarchy by other names — see the small tag under each rung.

Business Outcome Initiative / Theme

The result that justifies doing the work at all — the top of the stack, and the thing everything below has to trace back to.

Deliverables & Definition of Done Epic

What gets produced, and the sizing rule for when it's actually finished. For a first release, scope to an MLP (Minimum Lovable Product) rather than a bare MVP — the smallest version people actually want to use, not just the smallest version that technically works.

Work Packages User Story

Each deliverable split into ownable chunks — small enough that one person or team can own the whole thing.

Tasks Task / Sub-task

The individual, assignable units of work — the bottom of the stack, and the only level that actually shows up on someone's to-do list.

100%
The 100% Rule Applies at every level — the pieces below must add up to exactly the whole above. No gaps, no overlap. Same instinct as a fully-groomed Epic backlog: every bit of scope has a Story, nothing's been quietly dropped.
Owner Test Also applies at every level — if a piece doesn't have one clear owner, it's not decomposed enough yet. Keep splitting. Same discipline as a Story needing an assignee before it's "Ready."
Dependencies & Handover

The connective tissue between Work Packages and Tasks, not another rung — what blocks what, and who's waiting on whom. Same thing as "blocked by" links on a Story board, or a handoff at an Epic boundary between teams. Common failure modes: scope creep, splitting stories past the point they're still useful, and dependent owners left unclear until it's too late to matter.

MVP vs. MLP

An MVP asks "does it work?" An MLP asks "does it work, and would someone actually choose to use it?" Same discipline of cutting scope down to the smallest testable release — just with the delivery bar set a notch higher than bare functionality.

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I'm ready to break this project down into an execution hierarchy. Ask me what the Business Outcome is. Then walk me through Deliverables & Definition of Done, and split those into Work Packages small enough that one person can own each one completely — if a piece doesn't have a clear owner, split it further. Then break the first Work Package into Tasks. Flag anything that fails the 100% Rule — gaps or overlap between levels — and call out any dependencies I should track before handoff.

Ask me for my project's name, lead, and reporting period, then walk me through each section one at a time — milestones, what got done, budget numbers, risks, and next steps — and assemble it into the report once you have it.

Build the final report as a single self-contained HTML card, not a markdown or chat-only reply — a rounded card container on a soft neutral background, generous padding, dashed hairline rules between sections, small-caps gray eyebrow labels above each section title. Specifically:

  • Header: eyebrow "PROJECT STATUS REPORT", reporting period aligned top-right, project name as a bold headline, lead name and date range as a subtitle line.
  • Health Summary: a colored status dot + bold label + value for Project Health and Health Trend, plus Scope/Schedule/Budget shown as separate pill badges, each with its own status dot. Map Green/Amber/Red to a consistent muted color (not primary red/green/yellow) and never rely on color alone — always pair the dot with a text label.
  • Milestones & Achievements: two columns — a Milestones list with a filled dot for completed and a hollow dot for pending/in-progress, each showing planned → actual date; and a "Completed this period" list with checkmarks.
  • Task Progress: a donut/ring chart with the % complete as the center number, segmented into complete / at risk / not started, with a small legend giving exact percentages under it.
  • Planned vs. Actual: two horizontal progress bars stacked (Planned shown with a lighter hatched or outlined fill, Actual as a solid fill), each labeled with its percentage.
  • Budget Summary: a spend line ("$X of $Y spent" plus "Z% utilized") over a single horizontal utilization bar.
  • Key Risks & Issues: a warning-triangle icon beside each risk/issue, bolding the risk name, with a small severity tag.
  • Next Steps: an arrow icon beside each action, with owner and due date as a smaller line underneath.

Push back if I hand you a vague status instead of a real one, and don't round a flag up to Green just because I'd rather it looked that way.

A launch isn't done when the code ships — it's done when everyone who needs to know already does, before the customer finds out first. Six fields to work through, and the answer to the first one changes who else belongs in the room for the rest:

Launch Type — public (external customers) or internal (employees only)? This decides who else has to be in the room.

Audience Map — every group that needs to know, and by when: customers, internal teams, leadership, partners, press.

Communications Plan — if public, Marketing/PR owns the external announcement and press materials. If internal, the internal communications team owns the org-wide message. Either way, the project team doesn't freelance the announcement alone.

Customer Success Enablement — CS and support need to know before the customer does: talking points, known issues, escalation path, and training on what's changing. Not optional just because the launch is internal — internal launches still generate support tickets.

Rollout Sequencing — a phased rollout (beta → GA, or region by region) and what has to be true before moving to the next phase.

Post-Launch Monitoring — who's watching what in the first 24-72 hours, and what triggers a rollback.

The most common failure is treating communications as a checkbox at the very end instead of its own workstream with its own owner. By the time someone remembers Customer Success needed a heads-up, support tickets are already piling in from customers who found out before Support did.

Continuing the checkout-page example from Critical Path Method:

Launch Type: Public — this checkout page is customer-facing, so Marketing and PR are in scope, not just the project team.

Audience Map: Customers checking out today, merchants still on the old flow, the support team, and the exec who asked about conversion rate last QBR — four audiences, four different messages.

Communications Plan: Marketing drafts a one-line in-app banner for the new flow; PR sits this one out — a checkout redesign doesn't need a press release, just making sure nobody's surprised.

Customer Success Enablement: Support got a one-pager and a 15-minute walkthrough three days before launch, including the two known edge cases still being fixed in the next patch.

Rollout Sequencing: 5% of traffic for 48 hours, then 25%, then 100% — each step gated on conversion rate not dropping more than 1 point from baseline.

Post-Launch Monitoring: Checkout conversion rate and error-rate dashboards watched hourly for the first 72 hours; a 2-point conversion drop triggers an automatic rollback.

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I'm planning the launch of a product or feature and need a Launch & Communications Plan. Ask me whether this is a public launch (external customers) or an internal launch (employees only) first, then walk me through each field one at a time: Audience Map (every group that needs to know, and by when), Communications Plan (Marketing/PR for a public launch, or internal communications for an internal one — not the project team freelancing it), Customer Success Enablement (what Support needs to know before the customer does, including known issues and an escalation path), Rollout Sequencing (a phased rollout and what gates each phase), and Post-Launch Monitoring (what's being watched in the first 24-72 hours, and what triggers a rollback). Push back if Customer Success isn't mentioned at all, or if the plan skips straight to launch day with no phased rollout.

Breaking down silos, delivering value. Most projects pass through four handoffs — Strategy → Product → Engineering → Customer Success — and each one is a place work quietly falls through the cracks. What every handoff must include:

Context — why this work exists, in plain language.

Requirements — what "done" actually means to the receiving team.

Documentation — links to specs, decisions, and artifacts the receiving team needs, not tribal knowledge in someone's head.

Status — exactly where things stand right now, no rounding up.

Owner — a named person, not a team name.

Next Steps — the very next action, and who's taking it.

One team. One flow. One outcome.

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I'm handing this work off to another team. Walk me through what this handoff needs: Context (why this work exists, in plain language), Requirements (what "done" actually means to the receiving team), Documentation (links to specs, decisions, and artifacts they'll need — not tribal knowledge), Status (exactly where things stand right now, no rounding up), Owner (a named person, not a team name), and Next Steps (the very next action, and who's taking it). Push back if I leave Owner as a team name, or if Status is vaguer than "on track."
