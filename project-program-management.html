Project & Program Management

Planning a specific initiative before you run it — from a fuzzy idea to a chartered, resourced, trackable piece of work.

A good charter prevents a fuzzy project. Fill in these nine fields before work begins, and revisit it throughout the project for scope checks and trade-off decisions — not just once at kickoff:

  • Problem Statement — what's actually wrong, in one sentence.
  • Goal — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.
  • Business Case — why this is worth doing.
  • Scope — what's in, and just as important, what's out.
  • Tenets — short, memorable principles for how the team should decide when the charter doesn't spell it out. "Unless you know better ones" keeps them a guide, not a law.
  • Team — sponsor, team lead, process owner, stakeholders.
  • Timeline — the major milestones, not a full schedule.
  • Metrics — how you'll know it worked.
  • Risks — what could derail it.

Vague problem statements and weak sponsor alignment are the two most common ways a charter fails — a charter without a clearly accountable sponsor tends to stall the moment the project hits its first blocker.

Sample Goal: Reduce invoice approval cycle time from 10 days to 4 days within 90 days.

Sample Tenets (unless you know better ones):
1. "Default to self-service over a manual approval step."
2. "When speed and polish conflict before launch, speed wins."

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I'm kicking off a new project. Ask me about the problem I'm trying to solve, then walk me through drafting a Project Charter one field at a time — Problem Statement, Goal, Business Case, Scope, Tenets (short guiding principles for decisions the charter doesn't spell out — "unless you know better ones"), Team, Timeline, Metrics, and Risks. Push back if any of my answers are too vague.

A project plan is not project management strategy — the critical difference most project leaders miss. Strategy is the why: the operating logic for how the project creates value. Plan is the how: the execution roadmap that converts strategy into activities, owners, and milestones.

StrategyPlan
What it isHow the project creates value, aligns stakeholders, and adapts under uncertaintyThe roadmap that converts strategy into activities, owners, milestones, and controls
Time horizonProject lifecycle through benefits realization; reset at major stage gatesShort-term execution cycles — updated continuously by stage, sprint, or milestone
FlexibilityStable direction with tailored delivery optionsChanges frequently as execution conditions shift

Common mistakeTreating the schedule as the strategyOver-detailing tasks too early, tracking activity instead of outcomes

"People don't buy what you do, they buy why you do it."

— Simon Sinek, Start with Why

Start with Why is where the Strategy-is-the-why framing comes from — the same idea applies inside a project team, not just to customers. People don't follow the plan, they follow the reason behind it: a team that understands why a project matters can improvise its way through a plan that's wrong; a team that only has the plan is stuck the moment reality doesn't match it.

If "strategy" still feels slippery after that, Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy Bad Strategy is the sharpest explanation of why: most things called strategy are really just goals or vision statements in disguise. Real strategy, in his framing, is a diagnosis of the actual problem, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and a set of coherent actions that carry it out — worth reading before writing this section's Strategy column for real.

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I need to define the Strategy for a project, not just write a plan for it. If I have a Project Charter from earlier, I'll paste it in — use the Problem Statement and Business Case as a starting point, but push past them to the real diagnosis, not a goal in disguise. Otherwise, ask me what problem I'm actually trying to solve, then push me to name the why behind it before we touch any how. Once that's solid, help me write a guiding policy: the overall approach I'm choosing and why it beats the alternatives. Only then, sketch the first few coherent actions that follow from it, and call out anything that's really just a task list or a vision statement wearing a strategy costume.

Once Strategy has settled the why, this is where the how actually gets built: checkpoints along the way, a sequenced schedule between them, checked against real capacity, with a risk register for what could still break it.

Critical Path Method

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks in a project — the sequence that determines the earliest possible finish date. It's not always the path with the biggest individual tasks; it's whichever path adds up to the most days total. Every other path has slack — room to slip a little without moving the finish date.

Critical path (S→A→B→E) — 8 days, zero slack Parallel path (S→C→E) — 6 days, 2 days of slack

Those day counts have to come from somewhere. Most teams get them from Story Pointing — sizing each task's relative effort, often on a Fibonacci-ish scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13), then converting points to rough duration once the team's actual velocity is known. Get the points wrong and the whole critical path is fiction, however precise it looks on a chart.

Tracer Bullets

From The Pragmatic Programmer: before fully building out every task on the critical path, build a thin, working version that touches every node from Start to End — even if each piece is bare-bones. It proves the sequence and dependencies actually hold before real effort gets sunk into any one of them. Named for tracer ammunition: you see immediately whether you're aiming right, not just whether the gun fired.

Five ways to compress the critical path without losing quality:

  • Re-sequence tasks — adjust the order of dependent tasks to eliminate unnecessary waiting.
  • Overlap work carefully — start successor tasks before predecessors fully finish, without compromising quality.
  • Add resources selectively — assign another skilled contributor to the longest-pole tasks specifically.
  • Reduce scope where possible — cut or simplify non-essential features that sit on the critical path.
  • Remove approval delays — streamline review and approval steps that are blocking movement, not adding value.

Do not compress work blindly — quality and dependency logic still matter.

Reality Check

Capacity & Resource Planning Gotchas — not a step you complete once, a standing discipline you keep re-running against the schedule above. Revisit it weekly, not just at kickoff.

Planning at 100% capacity — leaves no room for interruptions. Fix: plan for 70–80% utilization instead.

Ignoring recurring meetings — overestimates available hours. Fix: account for standing meetings and reporting commitments.

Assuming all specialists are interchangeable — leads to skill gaps. Fix: plan by role and proficiency, not just headcount.

Missing vacation calendars — creates preventable schedule surprises. Fix: integrate time-off and holiday calendars into planning.

Not separating project work from BAU/KTLO — hides how much time is really available. Fix: track project work and business-as-usual work separately.

Overlooking key specialists — single points of failure go unnoticed. Fix: identify and calendar-block your bottleneck skill sets first.

Principles of effective capacity management: transparency, built-in buffers, realistic estimates, and a weekly review cadence.

Risk Management

Most projects don't need a five-lens enterprise risk framework — just an honest register and a real response. For each risk that could derail the plan: how likely is it, how bad if it happens, and what are you actually going to do about it?

  • Avoid — change the plan so the risk can't happen at all.
  • Mitigate — reduce the likelihood, the impact, or both.
  • Transfer — shift the risk elsewhere: a vendor contract, insurance, a more experienced team.
  • Accept — acknowledge it and move on, when managing it would cost more than the risk itself.

Run it as a cycle, not a one-time exercise: identify → assess → respond → monitor. The most common failure mode isn't missing a risk — it's writing it down once and never looking at it again.

For programs and larger initiatives where a single register isn't enough — five lenses to view risk through instead of one flat list:

  • Strategic Risk — threats to growth, market position, and long-term direction. Best for board and executive decisions.
  • Operational Resilience — disruption risk across processes, people, systems, and facilities. Best for continuity and service reliability.
  • Cyber & Data Risk — digital exposure from attacks, data loss, and technology failure. Best for security and control focus.
  • Third-Party Dependency — risk created by vendors, platforms, and critical partners. Best for supplier and ecosystem oversight.
  • AI & Model Risk — uncertainty from automated decisions and weak governance. Best for AI adoption and assurance.

Plan the logic before you commit to the dates. This isn't a checklist — it's a hierarchy. Start with the outcome that justifies the work, then keep splitting it into smaller, ownable pieces until you hit assignable tasks. If your team runs Agile, you already know this hierarchy by other names — see the small tag under each rung.

Business Outcome Initiative / Theme

The result that justifies doing the work at all — the top of the stack, and the thing everything below has to trace back to.

Deliverables & Definition of Done Epic

What gets produced, and the sizing rule for when it's actually finished. For a first release, scope to an MLP (Minimum Lovable Product) rather than a bare MVP — the smallest version people actually want to use, not just the smallest version that technically works.

Work Packages User Story

Each deliverable split into ownable chunks — small enough that one person or team can own the whole thing.

Tasks Task / Sub-task

The individual, assignable units of work — the bottom of the stack, and the only level that actually shows up on someone's to-do list.

100%
The 100% Rule Applies at every level — the pieces below must add up to exactly the whole above. No gaps, no overlap. Same instinct as a fully-groomed Epic backlog: every bit of scope has a Story, nothing's been quietly dropped.
Owner Test Also applies at every level — if a piece doesn't have one clear owner, it's not decomposed enough yet. Keep splitting. Same discipline as a Story needing an assignee before it's "Ready."
Dependencies & Handover

The connective tissue between Work Packages and Tasks, not another rung — what blocks what, and who's waiting on whom. Same thing as "blocked by" links on a Story board, or a handoff at an Epic boundary between teams. Common failure modes: scope creep, splitting stories past the point they're still useful, and dependent owners left unclear until it's too late to matter.

MVP vs. MLP

An MVP asks "does it work?" An MLP asks "does it work, and would someone actually choose to use it?" Same discipline of cutting scope down to the smallest testable release — just with the delivery bar set a notch higher than bare functionality.

Overview, track, communicate, succeed. A status report that actually gets read has the same shape every time:

  • Project Overview — name, lead, reporting period, overall status.
  • Health Summary — scope, schedule, and budget, each flagged on track / at risk / off track, plus an overall progress percentage.
  • Milestone Status — planned date, actual date, and comments per milestone.
  • Task Progress Summary — % complete, % at risk, % not started.
  • Budget Summary — total budget, spend to date, remaining, utilization.
  • Key Achievements — what actually got done this period.
  • Key Risks & Issues — what's threatening the plan right now.
  • Next Steps — action, owner, due date.

Breaking down silos, delivering value. Most projects pass through four handoffs — Strategy → Product → Engineering → Customer Success — and each one is a place work quietly falls through the cracks. What every handoff must include:

  • Context — why this work exists, in plain language.
  • Requirements — what "done" actually means to the receiving team.
  • Status — exactly where things stand right now, no rounding up.
  • Owner — a named person, not a team name.
  • Next Steps — the very next action, and who's taking it.

One team. One flow. One outcome.
