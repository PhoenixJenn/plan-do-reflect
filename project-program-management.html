Project & Program Management

Planning a specific initiative before you run it — from a fuzzy idea to a chartered, resourced, trackable piece of work.

A good charter prevents a fuzzy project. Fill in these nine fields before work begins, and revisit it throughout the project for scope checks and trade-off decisions — not just once at kickoff:

  • Problem Statement — what's actually wrong, in one sentence.
  • Goal — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.
  • Business Case — why this is worth doing.
  • Scope — what's in, and just as important, what's out.
  • Tenets — short, memorable principles for how the team should decide when the charter doesn't spell it out. "Unless you know better ones" keeps them a guide, not a law.
  • Team — sponsor, team lead, process owner, stakeholders.
  • Timeline — the major milestones, not a full schedule.
  • Metrics — how you'll know it worked.
  • Risks — what could derail it.

Vague problem statements and weak sponsor alignment are the two most common ways a charter fails — a charter without a clearly accountable sponsor tends to stall the moment the project hits its first blocker.

Sample Goal: Reduce invoice approval cycle time from 10 days to 4 days within 90 days.

Sample Tenets (unless you know better ones):
1. "Default to self-service over a manual approval step."
2. "When speed and polish conflict before launch, speed wins."

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I'm kicking off a new project. Ask me about the problem I'm trying to solve, then walk me through drafting a Project Charter one field at a time — Problem Statement, Goal, Business Case, Scope, Tenets, Team, Timeline, Metrics, and Risks. Push back if any of my answers are too vague.

A project plan is not project management strategy — the critical difference most project leaders miss. Strategy is the why: the operating logic for how the project creates value. Plan is the how: the execution roadmap that converts strategy into activities, owners, and milestones.

"People don't buy what you do, they buy why you do it."

— Simon Sinek, Start with Why

Start with Why is where the Strategy-is-the-why framing comes from — the same idea applies inside a project team, not just to customers. People don't follow the plan, they follow the reason behind it: a team that understands why a project matters can improvise its way through a plan that's wrong; a team that only has the plan is stuck the moment reality doesn't match it.

If "strategy" still feels slippery after that, Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy Bad Strategy is the sharpest explanation of why: most things called strategy are really just goals or vision statements in disguise. Real strategy, in his framing, is a diagnosis of the actual problem, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and a set of coherent actions that carry it out — worth reading before writing this section's Strategy column for real.

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever AI tool you use:

I need to define the Strategy for a project, not just write a plan for it. Ask me what problem I'm actually trying to solve — the real diagnosis, not a goal in disguise — then push me to name the why behind it before we touch any how. Once that's solid, help me write a guiding policy: the overall approach I'm choosing and why it beats the alternatives. Only then, sketch the first few coherent actions that follow from it, and call out anything that's really just a task list or a vision statement wearing a strategy costume.

Plan the logic before you commit to the dates. A complete decomposition stack — break outcomes into owned, testable work without losing the business intent:

  • The 100% Rule — the decomposed work must add up to exactly the full scope, no more, no less.
  • Business Outcome — the result that justifies doing the work at all.
  • Deliverables & Definition of Done — what gets produced, and the sizing rule for when it's actually finished. For a first release, scope to an MLP (Minimum Lovable Product) rather than a bare MVP — the smallest version people actually want to use, not just the smallest version that technically works.
  • Work Packages — the deliverable broken into ownable chunks.
  • Owner Test — if a work package doesn't have one clear owner, it's not decomposed enough yet.
  • Tasks — the individual, assignable units of work.
  • Dependencies & Handover — what blocks what, and who's waiting on whom.

Common failure modes: scope creep, splitting stories past the point they're still useful, and dependent owners left unclear until it's too late to matter.

MVP vs. MLP

An MVP asks "does it work?" An MLP asks "does it work, and would someone actually choose to use it?" Same discipline of cutting scope down to the smallest testable release — just with the delivery bar set a notch higher than bare functionality.

Smarter resource management starts with accurate assumptions and disciplined planning. The most common mistakes, and the fix for each:

  • Planning at 100% capacity — leaves no room for interruptions. Fix: plan for 70–80% utilization instead.
  • Ignoring recurring meetings — overestimates available hours. Fix: account for standing meetings and reporting commitments.
  • Assuming all specialists are interchangeable — leads to skill gaps. Fix: plan by role and proficiency, not just headcount.
  • Missing vacation calendars — creates preventable schedule surprises. Fix: integrate time-off and holiday calendars into planning.
  • Not separating project work from BAU/KTLO — hides how much time is really available. Fix: track project work and business-as-usual work separately.
  • Overlooking key specialists — single points of failure go unnoticed. Fix: identify and calendar-block your bottleneck skill sets first.

Principles of effective capacity management: transparency, built-in buffers, realistic estimates, and a weekly review cadence.

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks in a project — the sequence that determines the earliest possible finish date. Lose control of it, and the whole project slips with it. Five ways to compress it without losing quality:

  • Re-sequence tasks — adjust the order of dependent tasks to eliminate unnecessary waiting.
  • Overlap work carefully — start successor tasks before predecessors fully finish, without compromising quality.
  • Add resources selectively — assign another skilled contributor to the longest-pole tasks specifically.
  • Reduce scope where possible — cut or simplify non-essential features that sit on the critical path.
  • Remove approval delays — streamline review and approval steps that are blocking movement, not adding value.

Do not compress work blindly — quality and dependency logic still matter.

The decision discipline that protects objectives, value, resilience, and accountability when uncertainty changes the plan. Five lenses to view project risk through:

  • Strategic Risk — threats to growth, market position, and long-term direction. Best for board and executive decisions.
  • Operational Resilience — disruption risk across processes, people, systems, and facilities. Best for continuity and service reliability.
  • Cyber & Data Risk — digital exposure from attacks, data loss, and technology failure. Best for security and control focus.
  • Third-Party Dependency — risk created by vendors, platforms, and critical partners. Best for supplier and ecosystem oversight.
  • AI & Model Risk — uncertainty from automated decisions and weak governance. Best for AI adoption and assurance.

Run it as a cycle, not a one-time exercise: Sense signals → Assess exposure → Choose a response → Track treatment → Report assurance. The most common failure mode isn't missing a risk — it's recording it and then never managing it.

Overview, track, communicate, succeed. A status report that actually gets read has the same shape every time:

  • Project Overview — name, lead, reporting period, overall status.
  • Health Summary — scope, schedule, and budget, each flagged on track / at risk / off track, plus an overall progress percentage.
  • Milestone Status — planned date, actual date, and comments per milestone.
  • Task Progress Summary — % complete, % at risk, % not started.
  • Budget Summary — total budget, spend to date, remaining, utilization.
  • Key Achievements — what actually got done this period.
  • Key Risks & Issues — what's threatening the plan right now.
  • Next Steps — action, owner, due date.

Breaking down silos, delivering value. Most projects pass through four handoffs — Strategy → Product → Engineering → Customer Success — and each one is a place work quietly falls through the cracks. What every handoff must include:

  • Context — why this work exists, in plain language.
  • Requirements — what "done" actually means to the receiving team.
  • Status — exactly where things stand right now, no rounding up.
  • Owner — a named person, not a team name.
  • Next Steps — the very next action, and who's taking it.

One team. One flow. One outcome.
